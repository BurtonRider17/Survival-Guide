Part 1Planning: Shelter First, Then a Home

1.1 From emergency shelter to permanent home

In a grid-down emergency, people die of exposure long before they die of hunger. Hypothermia can kill in hours in wet, windy weather at temperatures well above freezing; heat stroke can kill in a day in the desert. So housing is built in stages: get out of the wind and wet tonight, get a warm, dry, defended room this season, and build a durable house over one to three years. Each stage buys time to do the next one properly.

Fix before you buildThe fastest permanent home is usually an existing building. A sound house with a good roof can be made livable by heating one or two rooms (close off the rest, hang blankets over doorways, add a wood stove with a proper chimney), fixing the roof, and adding insulation to the ceiling. Build new when no safe building exists, when the location is wrong (flood plain, too exposed, no water), or once you have time and materials.
Survival shelter checklist
  • Site: dry, out of the wind, not in a gully or dry wash (flash floods), not under dead trees or limbs (“widow-makers”)
  • Insulate from the ground first; then roof; then walls. Small is warm — just big enough to lie down
  • Ventilate any shelter with a fire or stove; never burn charcoal or run a generator or camp stove in a closed tent (carbon monoxide)
  • Snow shelters: keep a vent hole open, keep a shovel inside, mark the outside so others can find you
  • Upgrade daily: thicker roof, door plug, raised bed, drainage trench around the outside

1.2 Choosing a site

A good site makes every later job easier; a bad site cannot be fixed by good carpentry. Walk the land in the worst weather you can, and talk to anyone who knows its history of floods, fires and drifts. Rank the factors below — water and safety from floods come first.

1.3 Sizing a small house and simple floor plans

Every square foot must be built, roofed, heated and maintained with hand tools. Build small, build it well, and design so you can add on later. A useful rule is 100–200 ft² (9–19 m²) per person for the heated core, plus unheated storage, porch and sheds. A loft under a steep roof adds sleeping space almost for free.

1.4 Passive solar orientation

A well-oriented house heats itself partly with winter sun and stays cooler in summer — free energy you never have to cut, haul or split. The U.S. DOE’s passive solar guide lists five elements: aperture (south windows within 30° of true south, unshaded 9 a.m.–3 p.m. in winter), absorber (a dark surface the sun hits), thermal mass (masonry, earth, stone or water that stores heat), distribution (natural airflow to other rooms), and control (overhangs, shutters, vents).

Part 2Tools, Timber & Salvage

2.1 Hand tools

A house can be built with no electricity at all — every log cabin, timber frame and adobe on the continent was, until about 1900. Buy or salvage good steel tools now, along with files and stones to keep them sharp; a dull tool is slow and dangerous. Old American and Canadian tools from estate sales are often better steel than cheap new ones.

2.2 Felling and working timber

Felling is one of the most dangerous jobs there isLogging has one of the highest fatality rates of any occupation (OSHA). Never fell alone; never fell in strong wind; clear brush around the trunk and plan two escape routes; watch for dead limbs overhead; never work under a hung-up tree — pull it down with a rope and come-along from a distance. With a chainsaw wear a helmet with face screen, hearing protection, chaps, gloves and boots. Bucking on slopes: always stand uphill of the log.

  • When to cut: late fall and winter — less sap, fewer insects, slower stain and rot, logs skid easily on snow.
  • Peel promptly: remove bark within weeks (it peels easiest in spring/early summer). Bark harbors beetles and traps moisture. Stack peeled logs off the ground on cross-skids, covered on top.
  • Drying: air-dry sawn lumber stacked flat with sticks (“stickers”) every 2 ft, top covered, sides open; roughly one year per inch of thickness for hardwoods (less for softwoods). Logs and green timber frames are traditionally built green and allowed to dry in place — plan for shrinkage.
  • Shrinkage: wood shrinks very little along its length but several percent across the grain as it dries (USDA Wood Handbook). A green log wall settles — commonly several inches on an 8 ft wall — so leave settling space above doors and windows (see Part 4).
  • Hewing: snap chalk lines on both sides of the log, “score” cuts every 4–6 in with a felling axe down to the line, knock off the chunks (“juggling”), then smooth to the line with a broadaxe.
  • Splitting boards: straight-grained logs can be split into planks with wedges and a froe (riving); this was how fences, shakes and floor puncheons were made before sawmills.

2.3 Salvaging materials safely

Abandoned buildings hold enormous value: framing lumber, metal roofing, windows, doors, hardware, nails and screws, wire, pipe, insulation, brick and stone. Take buildings apart in the reverse of the order they were built — roofing, then sheathing, rafters, walls, floors — and never pull out a support while something is resting on it.

Asbestos — do not break it, do not breathe itMany North American buildings from before about 1980 contain asbestos (EPA). Inhaled fibers cause asbestosis, lung cancer and mesothelioma decades later; there is no treatment. Assume it is present in: vermiculite attic insulation (grey-brown pebbles), pipe and boiler insulation, 9×9 in floor tiles and black mastic, popcorn ceilings, textured plaster and joint compound, cement siding and roof shingles (“transite”), roofing felts, window putty, duct wrap. Intact material left in place is low risk. If you must move it: wet it thoroughly first, do not saw, sand, drill or break it, wear a P100 / HEPA respirator (paper dust masks and plain N95s are not adequate), disposable coveralls, and bag it wet. Best of all, salvage elsewhere.
Lead paintHomes built before 1978 probably contain lead paint (CDC). Lead dust damages the brain and nerves, especially in children and pregnant women; there is no safe blood level. Do not dry-sand, power-plane, heat-gun or burn painted wood. Use EPA lead-safe practices: mist with water and hand-scrape, contain debris on plastic sheeting, wear a P100 respirator, wash hands and face before eating, keep children away, and clean with wet wipes and a HEPA vacuum — never dry-sweep. Plane or saw old painted boards only outdoors, wet, or remove the painted surface first. Never burn painted wood or pressure-treated lumber (older treated wood contains arsenic — CCA).
Structural collapse and other hazardsDo not enter buildings with sagging roof lines, leaning or cracked walls, fire damage, flood-soaked floors, or after an earthquake until you have shored them. Never work alone; keep someone outside who knows where you are. Wear a hard hat, boots with stiff soles (nails), gloves and eye protection; keep tetanus vaccination current. Watch for gas (propane tanks, buried lines), live wires if any power remains, rotten floors over basements, wasp and animal nests. Rodent droppings: hantavirus is spread in the dust — air the building out, soak droppings with a bleach solution, wipe up, and never sweep or vacuum them dry (CDC). Mold: wear a respirator; discard soaked insulation and drywall.

Part 3Foundations

The foundation does three jobs: it carries the weight of the house to firm ground, it keeps wood and earth walls dry by lifting them above splash and ground moisture, and in cold climates it keeps frost heave from lifting and cracking the building. The companion Survival Info: Concrete & Masonry volume covers mixing concrete and lime, making brick, and laying stone and block in depth; this part gives the basics you need to choose and lay out a foundation.

3.2 Frost depth by region

Frost heave happens when water in fine-grained soil freezes into ice lenses that lift whatever is above. Footings must go below the deepest frost penetration, onto soil that drains, or be protected by insulation. Footings in every climateEven where it never freezes, dig below topsoil and roots to firm, undisturbed soil — at least 12 in (30 cm) — and make the footing about twice as wide as the wall it carries (wider on soft clay). Foundation layout and build sequence

  • Dig footings to firm soil and below the frost line; keep trench bottoms flat and free of loose soil
  • Lay drain (stone + perforated pipe, or a stone-filled French drain) sloping to daylight at least 1:100
  • Build footing and wall/piers; bring top at least 8 in (20 cm) above finished grade
  • Earthquake and hurricane zones: embed anchors — ½ in (12 mm) bolts about every 6 ft (1.8 m) and within 12 in (30 cm) of each corner and end are a common code minimum

Part 4Wall Systems

Choose a wall system by what is abundant where you are (trees, straw, clay, stone, salvage), the climate (cold needs insulation; hot-dry likes mass; hot-humid likes light, ventilated walls), and the hazards (earthquake zones punish heavy unreinforced walls; termites punish wood near the ground). All walls share two rules: a good hat and good boots — a wide roof overhang and a foundation that keeps the wall above splash and ground water.

4.2 Log cabins: notching and chinking

The log cabin is the classic North American wilderness home because it needs nothing but trees, an axe and a saw. The USDA Forest Service’s Building with Logs (1945) is still a good free manual. Choose straight, evenly sized logs; alternate butt ends and top ends on each course so the walls rise level; and keep the bottom logs (sill logs) on a stone or concrete foundation at least 8 in (20 cm) above the ground — rot starts at the bottom.

  1. Sill course: lay the two side sills, then the two end logs notched over them. Flatten the bottom of sill logs; set on a capillary break.
  2. Raise courses: roll logs up skids with ropes (parbuckling) or a gin pole. Notch each log to fit the one below; check the wall for plumb and level every course.
  3. Openings: build the wall solid, then saw out door and window openings, and spike or peg vertical boards (bucks) to the log ends to hold them.
  4. Settling space: leave a gap of about 2–3 in (5–8 cm) above every door and window frame (more for very green logs), filled loosely with insulation and covered with trim attached to the frame only — never nail the frame to the logs above.
  5. Roof: extend the top logs (plates) as overhangs; use rafters, purlins on log gables, or ridge-pole construction (Part 5).
  6. Chink and finish: after the first summer of drying, chink gaps and treat log ends; re-chink as needed. Oils and borate treatments slow decay.
How many logs?A 16 × 24 ft cabin with 8 ft walls of 10 in logs needs about 10–12 courses × 4 walls ≈ 45–50 wall logs, plus gable logs or framing, rafters, joists and purlins: plan on 60–100 trees. Cut in winter, peel, and let them season for a few months off the ground if you can.

4.3 Timber framing basics

A timber frame is a skeleton of large posts and beams (typically 6×6 to 8×10 in) locked together with wooden joints and pegs, and braced diagonally. It needs fewer trees than a log house and lasts for centuries; the walls between the posts can be filled with whatever you have — straw-clay, straw bales, stud walls, brick nogging or wattle and daub. Frames are laid out on the ground in bents (cross-sections), raised with ropes and pike poles by a crew, then tied together with girts and plates.

Timber-frame rules of thumb
  • Bents at 8–12 ft (2.4–3.7 m) spacing; keep spans modest for the timber you have
  • Mortises no wider than ⅓ of the timber thickness; tenons 1½–2 in thick; leave 4 in+ of wood beyond a peg hole
  • Pegs of straight-grained, dry oak or locust, ¾–1 in (19–25 mm), split rather than sawn
  • Brace every post in both directions at corners; in earthquake country add hold-downs to the foundation
  • Cut joints green; dry timbers shrink — keep critical surfaces where shrinkage tightens rather than loosens the joint

4.4 Stick framing with salvaged lumber

Light wood framing with 2×4s and 2×6s (“stick framing”) is the fastest way to build if you have nails and a supply of lumber from abandoned buildings. It is described step by step in U.S. Army FM 5-426 Carpentry and CMHC’s Canadian Wood-Frame House Construction.

4.5 Cordwood masonry

Cordwood walls are built like a stack of firewood laid in mortar: short, debarked log rounds (typically 12–24 in / 30–60 cm long) set crosswise in the wall. Each mortar bed is split into an outer and inner bead with a core of insulation (sawdust treated with lime, or loose insulation) between them. They use wood too small or crooked for log building and are popular in Wisconsin, Ontario and the Northeast.

  • Use dry, debarked, rot-resistant softwood (cedar, tamarack, pine, spruce); dry for a year so the wood shrinks before it is set — wet wood shrinks away from the mortar and leaves gaps.
  • Typical mortar: sand, sawdust (soaked), lime and Portland cement — e.g., roughly 9 sand : 3 sawdust : 3 lime : 2 cement by volume. Sawdust slows curing and reduces shrinkage cracking. See the Concrete & Masonry volume for lime.
  • Build within a post-and-beam frame or use stacked “cordwood corners” (crisscrossed squared timbers). Protect with a wide overhang; keep the first course well above splash.

4.6 Straw bale

Straw — the dry stalks left after a grain harvest, not hay — baled tightly makes a thick, well-insulated wall for very little money. Straw bale construction has had its own appendix in the U.S. International Residential Code since 2015 (Appendix S/AS; Appendix BJ in 2024). Bales are either load-bearing (the roof sits on a wooden box beam on top of the bales) or infill in a post-and-beam frame (safer for beginners and heavy snow).

Straw bale essentials
  • Bales: wheat, rice, oat, barley or rye straw, dense and dry — moisture content below about 20% when built; reject moldy or hot bales
  • Raise bales on a foundation at least 8 in (20 cm) above grade, on a toe-up (wood sill with gravel between) and a moisture barrier underneath only
  • Stack in running bond; pin and compress; stuff gaps with loose straw or straw-clay
  • Plaster both sides with clay or lime plaster (2–3 coats, 1–2 in total); never use plastic sheeting or cement-only stucco that traps moisture
  • Roof overhang 18–24 in (45–60 cm) or more; gutters; flash window sills carefully — most failures start at leaky sills
  • Fire: plastered bale walls perform well in fire tests; loose straw on site is a major fire hazard — keep it swept and no smoking

4.7 Earthen walls: cob, adobe, earthbag, rammed earth

Earthen walls use the subsoil under your feet. They are fireproof, pest-resistant, cool in hot-dry climates and nearly free — but heavy, slow, poor insulators, and dangerous in earthquakes if unreinforced. Test your soil: shake a jar one-third full of subsoil with water and a pinch of salt, let it settle a day, and read the layers (sand at the bottom, silt, then clay on top). A good building soil is roughly 15–30% clay with the rest sand and silt; too much clay cracks, too little crumbles.

4.8 Stone walls

Stone lasts forever and is often free in New England, the Ozarks, the Appalachians, the Rockies and much of Mexico. Lay stone with the longest dimension into the wall, break joints (each stone covering a joint below), use frequent “through stones” that cross the full wall thickness, and keep walls at least 45 cm (18 in) thick. Lime mortar is traditional and kinder to soft stone (NPS Brief 2); details are in the Concrete & Masonry volume. Stone is a poor insulator and feels cold and damp — in cold climates insulate the inside (a stud wall with insulation, or a straw-clay lining), or use stone for the foundation, chimney and a sun-warmed mass wall only.

5.3 Roof pitch for rain and snow

Snow, wind and upliftSnow can weigh 5–20 lb/ft³: a meter of wet spring snow on a roof can exceed 100 psf (5 kPa). Shovel or rake roofs before loads exceed what you built for, starting at the eaves, and never stand under a sliding snow load. In hurricane and tornado country the danger is the opposite — wind lifts roofs off. Use hip roofs (4 sloping sides) at 4:12–6:12, keep overhangs modest (≤ 18–24 in), and tie every rafter to the wall and the wall to the foundation with metal straps, wire or bolts (FEMA P-499). A roof is only as strong as its weakest fastener.

5.5 Gutters and rainwater catchment

  • Gutters can be made from salvaged metal bent into a half-pipe or V-trough, PVC pipe sawn in half, or two boards nailed in a V and tarred. Slope about 1:200 (1/16 in per ft) to the downspout; hang them so the roof edge drips into the middle of the gutter.
  • Size the storage: 1 inch of rain on 1,000 ft² of roof ≈ 623 US gallons (1 mm on 1 m² = 1 L). A 16 × 24 ft cabin with 2 ft overhangs (≈ 560 ft² roof) in 30 in/yr of rain yields about 0.623 × 560 × 30 × 0.8 ≈ 8,400 gal/yr.
  • Roof materials for drinking water: metal, slate, tile and clean membranes are best; avoid lead flashing, treated-wood shakes, old asphalt roofs, and copper for drinking water.
  • Covered, opaque tanks with screened inlets and overflows stop mosquitoes and algae. Always filter and disinfect rainwater for drinking — see the Water volume.

Part 6Doors, Windows, Insulation & Moisture

6.1 Doors

The simplest strong door is a board-and-batten (ledged and braced) door: vertical tongue-and-groove or butted boards held by two or three horizontal ledges and a diagonal brace in a Z. The diagonal must run upward from the bottom hinge side toward the latch side so it is in compression and holds the door from sagging. Hang it on long strap hinges with bolts or clinched nails. In cold climates, double up the boards with felt or insulation between them, and add weatherstrip (felt, rubber, leather) around the stop.

Door and entry checklist
  • Outswing doors resist wind-driven rain and forced entry better in hurricane areas; inswing doors can't be blocked by snow drifts — choose for your climate
  • Solid door + steel bar in brackets on the inside for security; hinges on the inside or with non-removable pins
  • Airlock/mudroom entry (two doors) in cold climates cuts heat loss and keeps snow out
  • Every bedroom has a second way out: a window at least ~20 in wide × 24 in high (5.7 ft² clear opening is the U.S. code size) with a sill no higher than 44 in

6.2 Windows, salvaged glass and shutters

Glass is one of the few building materials you cannot practically make by hand, so salvage every intact window and pane you can: from abandoned houses, cars (tempered glass — cannot be cut), shower doors, picture frames, and greenhouse panels. Keep salvaged sashes whole and build the rough opening to fit them; the frame is easier to make than the window.

  • Install: rough opening ½ in (12 mm) larger than the window; sloped sill; flashing or tar-paper “shingled” so water runs out over the siding; header above (see 4.4). In log walls, allow settling space above.
  • Double glazing: two salvaged panes in one frame with a 12–20 mm (½–¾ in) sealed air gap roughly halves heat loss compared with a single pane. A storm sash or tight plastic film on the inside does nearly as well.
  • Cutting glass: score once with a wheel cutter along a straightedge, tap under the score, snap over a dowel. Wear gloves and eye protection. Tempered glass shatters into cubes — use it whole.
  • No glass? Oiled paper or muslin, polycarbonate or clear plastic film.
Insulating shuttersWindows lose 5–10 times more heat than an insulated wall. Interior shutters or panels of 1–2 in rigid foam, wool or straw-filled boxes, fitted tightly into the window frame at night, can cut window heat loss dramatically and also keep lamplight from showing outside at night. Exterior plank shutters with a bar protect glass from hurricanes, hail and intruders; in hurricane zones use plywood ≥ ⅝ in (16 mm) fastened to the framing.

6.3 Insulation from natural and salvaged materials

Insulation works by trapping still, dry air. Anything fluffy, dry and pest-proof insulates; anything dense (stone, earth, concrete) does not. The ceiling/roof comes first (warm air rises), then walls, then floors and foundations. Stop air leaks before adding insulation — a gap the width of a pencil around a window can lose as much heat as the wall around it.

6.4 Ventilation and moisture control

Most old houses rot from water, not age. Moisture comes from rain (the largest source), ground moisture, and indoor air — people, cooking, drying clothes and unvented heaters all add water vapor. Building Science Corporation’s rule: keep water out, and when it does get in, let it dry out (BSD-106, BSD-013).

Moisture control checklist
  • Wide roof overhangs, gutters, and ground sloping away from the house; splash blocks under downspouts
  • Foundation top ≥ 8 in (20 cm) above grade; capillary break under sills and earthen/bale walls
  • Rainscreen gap behind siding; flashing at every window, door and roof penetration — lap every layer like shingles
  • Ground cover in crawl spaces: overlapping plastic sheet or a thick gravel layer; vents (about 1 ft² per 150 ft² of crawl area) or a sealed, insulated crawl
  • Cold climates: air-tight, vapor-retarding layer on the warm (inside) face; hot-humid climates: no interior plastic — let walls dry inward
  • Ventilate roofs or attics (eave + ridge vents) to remove moisture and prevent ice dams
  • Keep indoor relative humidity about 30–50% (EPA): vent cooking and bathing moisture, dry clothes outdoors or near the stove with a window cracked
  • Fix leaks within 24–48 hours; dry or discard anything moldy (EPA)
Fresh air and radonA tight house with a wood stove, oil lamps and several people needs fresh air: open windows briefly twice a day (a few minutes of cross-draft), and give the stove its own outside combustion-air inlet if possible. Radon, a radioactive soil gas and the second-leading cause of lung cancer in the U.S. (EPA), builds up in tight basements and earth-sheltered rooms — ventilate them and seal floor cracks.

A cookstove with an oven and water reservoir is ideal for a family.

7.2 Chimney safety and clearances

Stovepipe and chimney checklist
  • Stovepipe 24-gauge or heavier; male (crimped) ends point down toward the stove so creosote runs back inside; three sheet-metal screws per joint
  • Pipe rises toward the chimney at least ¼ in per foot; keep horizontal runs short (≤ ~75% of the vertical height) with no more than two 90° elbows
  • Pass through walls and ceilings only with a listed thimble or insulated Class A chimney section — never bare single-wall pipe through wood
  • Chimney 3 ft above the roof penetration and 2 ft higher than anything within 10 ft; spark-arrestor cap (essential in wildfire country and under thatch or shakes)
  • Install a damper or barometric control; never close it fully while a fire is burning
  • Inspect monthly in heating season and sweep when creosote is ⅛ in (3 mm) thick or at least yearly (CSIA). Keep a brush, rods and ladder
  • Battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm and smoke alarm in every sleeping area; fire extinguisher or water and a bucket of sand by the stove
  • Store ashes in a lidded metal can outdoors, away from the house, for several days — they stay hot
Chimney fires and carbon monoxideA roaring sound, shaking pipe, sparks or flames from the chimney top mean a chimney fire: close the stove air inlets and damper, get everyone out, and spray the roof around the chimney. Afterward inspect for cracks before using it again. CO from a smoldering or back-drafting stove is odorless — headache, nausea, dizziness or confusion that improve outdoors mean CO poisoning (EPA). Never use a charcoal grill, camp stove, gas oven or generator for heat indoors.

7.3 Rocket mass heaters

A rocket mass heater (RMH) burns small sticks very completely and stores the heat in a massive bench, so it can heat a small house on a fraction of the wood of an ordinary stove. Masonry heaters (Russian/Finnish stoves) work on the same principle and are proven over centuries. RMHs are home-built, experimental appliances: Oregon’s Alternative Technology Advisory Committee found they are not covered by recognized codes and recommended applying masonry-heater and wood-stove safety requirements. Learn from an experienced builder, follow a published design exactly (e.g., Ianto Evans & Leslie Jackson; Ernie & Erica Wisner), use clean firebrick and insulating refractory in the burn area, provide clean-outs, test the draft with the bench open, and install a CO alarm.

Daylight first: south windows, a skylight or a clerestory over the work area, light-colored interior walls (whitewash — lime and water — is cheap, bright and mildew-resistant), and a work table by a window. Flame lighting is a major fire riskCandles start thousands of home fires every year (NFPA). Keep flames 12 in (30 cm) from anything that can burn, in a stable holder with a guard, never near curtains, bedding, straw or thatch, never unattended or in a room where people sleep, and out of reach of children and pets. Store fuel outside the living space. Keep a fire extinguisher or bucket of water ready and plan a fire escape route (Ready.gov).

Part 8Sanitation

Human waste spreads cholera, typhoid, dysentery, hepatitis A and parasites — historically, poor sanitation has killed more people in disasters than the disasters themselves. Separate waste from drinking water, food and flies from day one. The WHO Guidelines on Sanitation and Health stress that every toilet must safely contain waste and keep it away from people, water and insects.

8.1 The outhouse (pit privy)

Outhouse construction
  • Site: well-drained, never in a flood zone or where water collects; 30 m (100 ft) or more from wells and surface water, downhill; 15–30 m from the house (close enough to use at night, far enough for smell and flies)
  • Pit about 1–1.5 m (3–5 ft) deep, 1 × 1.2 m (3 × 4 ft); line the top 50 cm or more with logs, stone or boards so the edges don't cave in
  • Mound soil around the base so rain flows away; set the outhouse on a sill or slab larger than the pit
  • Tight seat with a lid; fly-screened vent pipe from the pit up past the roof (a dark pipe in the sun draws air upward — this is the VIP latrine principle)
  • After each use add a scoop of wood ash, sawdust, dry soil or lime to cut odor and flies; no trash, no chemicals
  • When waste is within ~50 cm (18 in) of the top, fill the pit with soil, mound it, plant it with a non-food shrub, and move the house to a new pit
  • Handwashing station (tippy-tap, soap and water) right outside; this single step prevents much of the disease spread

8.2 Composting toilets

Where the water table is high, the ground is rocky or frozen, or you want to recover nutrients, use a bucket composting toilet (described in detail in Joseph Jenkins’s Humanure Handbook). A 5-gallon bucket sits under a wooden seat box; after each use, cover the deposit fully with a carbon material — sawdust, peat, chopped leaves or straw — so there is no smell and no flies. Full buckets are emptied into an outdoor compost bin and covered with more carbon material.

  • Use at least two or three bins (about 1 m³ each), on soil, roofed or covered, away from water sources. Fill one for a year, then leave it to age while you fill the next.
  • Hot (thermophilic) composting kills most pathogens; a well-built pile reaches 50–65 °C (120–150 °F). Check with a thermometer if you have one. Age finished material at least a further year after the last addition; if unsure, use it only on trees and non-food plants.
  • Separating urine (a funnel to a separate jug) reduces smell and bulk; urine diluted about 1:5–1:10 with water is a good nitrogen fertilizer for non-leafy crops, applied to soil, not leaves.
  • Wash buckets with water and a little soap or ash; pour rinse water on the pile.

8.3 Greywater

Greywater is used water from washing, bathing and laundry — not toilets (blackwater). It still contains bacteria, grease, soap and food scraps, but used carefully it can water trees and ornamental plants (NMSU M-106; Oregon DEQ; Utah State Extension).

Greywater rules
  • Use it within 24 hours — never store it (it goes septic and smells within a day or two)
  • Discharge below a 5–10 cm layer of mulch or into a mulch-filled basin around trees, not onto the surface or onto lawns where people walk
  • Never on root crops or leafy vegetables eaten raw; never into streams, wells or ditches; keep well back from wells (30 m / 100 ft is a common setback)
  • Use plant-friendly soaps: low sodium and boron, no bleach; strain out food and grease (kitchen-sink water is the dirtiest)
  • Rotate discharge points so the soil never stays saturated; stop in wet weather or when ground is frozen
  • A simple grease trap (bucket with a baffle) and a gravel-and-sand filter before the mulch basin extends its life
Diapers, illness and septic systemsIf someone has diarrheal disease, treat all their waste and washing water as blackwater: compost hot or bury deep, wash hands thoroughly, and keep it far from water and gardens. If you live in a house with an intact septic system, it will keep working without power for gravity-fed systems if you limit water use and keep grease and chemicals out (EPA Septic).

1Arctic & Subarctic

Best methods & materials: Log (spruce, birch for interior) in the forested subarctic; super-insulated stick frame (double stud walls with 10–14 in of cellulose, sawdust or salvaged batts) where lumber is available; traditional sod-and-driftwood or earth-bermed houses on the treeless coast. Small, compact, two-story or loft; arctic entry; small north windows, larger south windows (low sun).

Climate hazards: −40 to −50° cold, months of darkness, permafrost, strong winds and drifting, wildfire in the boreal forest, very large earthquakes in southern Alaska, short building season.

Insulation & design: The highest anywhere: ceilings R-60+, walls R-40+, floor R-30+ over a ventilated crawl. Post-and-pad or piles with adjustable jacks on a gravel pad; keep the ground frozen (CCHRC). Insulated, heat-traced or buried water lines; keep the stove going.

2Pacific NW & BC

Best methods & materials: Abundant timber: stick frame, timber frame and log; western red cedar for shakes, siding and sills. East of the Cascades and in the BC interior: log and straw bale. Big roofs with 2–3 ft overhangs and gutters are essential west of the mountains.

Climate hazards: Constant winter rain and rot; mold; the Cascadia subduction zone (magnitude-9 earthquake and coastal tsunami), landslides on steep wet slopes, wildfire and drought in the interior and late summer; heavy mountain snow.

Insulation & design: Moderate (walls R-20, ceiling R-38–49); rainscreen siding and breathable walls. Wood-frame walls well braced and bolted to footings for earthquakes; avoid unreinforced masonry, cob and adobe on the coast. Build above tsunami inundation zones on the outer coast.

3Prairies & Great Plains

Best methods & materials: Straw bale (abundant wheat straw — historic Nebraska Sandhills bale houses still stand), earth-bermed or earth-sheltered houses into south-facing slopes, rammed earth; sod houses were the original prairie homes. Timber is scarce — salvage lumber from towns and farm buildings; use river-bottom cottonwood only for temporary work.

Climate hazards: Tornadoes, blizzards and ground blizzards, −30 to −40° cold in the north, very high winds, hail, drought and grass fires, scarce firewood.

Insulation & design: High (walls R-30+, ceilings R-49–60); passive solar heating is very effective under the sunny winter skies. Every house needs a tornado shelter: a below-ground cellar or a reinforced room. Dense shelterbelts on the north and west; low profile, strong roof ties.

4Great Lakes & Midwest

Best methods & materials: Log and timber frame from hardwood and pine forests in the north; cordwood (historic in Wisconsin and Ontario); straw bale; stick frame from the vast stock of abandoned buildings in towns. Fieldstone and brick for foundations and chimneys.

Climate hazards: Cold snowy winters, lake-effect snow belts (Michigan, Ontario, NY), ice storms, tornadoes, spring river floods (Mississippi, Ohio, Red River), humid summers; the New Madrid seismic zone in Missouri, Arkansas, Tennessee and Kentucky.

Insulation & design: Walls R-20–30, ceilings R-49; vapor retarder on the warm side in the north. Snow-load rafters (Part 5) in lake belts. Build above flood levels; brace and tie houses in the New Madrid zone; tornado cellar in the southern part.

5Northeast & Atlantic Canada

Best methods & materials: Timber frame (the New England tradition), log in the north woods, fieldstone everywhere, cordwood; abundant salvage in the dense urban corridor. Steep roofs (8:12–12:12) with metal or cedar shakes shed heavy wet snow.

Climate hazards: Long cold winters, nor'easters, ice storms that bring down trees, hurricanes tracking up the coast, coastal flooding, heavy wet snow, humid summers, deep frost.

Insulation & design: Walls R-20–30, ceilings R-49; airlock entries. Strong rafters and snow raking; tie roofs down on the coast. Place the house away from tall trees that can fall on it in ice storms; stone and brick need inside insulation.

6Southeast & Gulf

Best methods & materials: Raised wood frame houses on brick or stone piers (good airflow, flood and termite protection), with metal roofs, deep porches and high ceilings — the “dogtrot” and “shotgun” traditions; brick where clay is abundant; log in the Appalachians and Ozarks.

Climate hazards: Hurricanes on the Gulf and Atlantic coasts, tornadoes (“Dixie Alley”), extreme humidity, mold, very heavy subterranean termite pressure (Formosan termites on the Gulf coast), river and coastal floods, ice storms inland.

Insulation & design: Moderate (walls R-13–20, ceiling R-30–38) plus shade, ventilation and a light-colored or radiant-barrier roof. No interior plastic vapor barrier. Termite shields, durable heartwood or treated sills, keep wood 18 in (45 cm) off the ground and inspect for mud tubes. Hurricane straps and hip roofs on the coast.

7Florida

Best methods & materials: The Florida “cracker house”: a raised wood frame on piers with a steep metal roof, wide porches, a central breezeway and windows on all sides; or concrete block (CBS) where cement is available (see Concrete & Masonry). Hip roofs strapped to walls.

Climate hazards: Category 4–5 hurricanes and storm surge, frequent flooding, Formosan and drywood termites, heat and humidity, lightning, sinkholes in limestone areas, no frost.

Insulation & design: Roof insulation and shade matter most; minimal wall insulation. Build well above surge and flood levels (FEMA P-499) on deep piers; shutters for every opening; continuous load path from roof to foundation. Termite-resistant wood or steel; never leave wood debris near the house.

8Desert SW & Great Basin

Best methods & materials: Adobe and rammed earth (Pueblo and Hispanic traditions), straw bale with earthen or lime plaster, earthbag domes and walls, stone. Thick mass walls; flat earthen roofs on vigas are traditional but need constant maintenance — a pitched metal roof over mass walls is more forgiving.

Climate hazards: Extreme summer heat with cold nights, flash floods in arroyos, water scarcity, intense sun and UV, wildfire, earthquakes along the Wasatch Front and in Nevada; desert subterranean termites; cold snowy winters in the Great Basin and high desert.

Insulation & design: Low desert: mass walls, deep shade, light roofs, night ventilation, ceiling insulation R-30–38. High desert/Great Basin: insulated mass (bale or insulated adobe) and passive solar. Keep earthen walls on stone footings with drainage. Seismic bond beams in Utah and Nevada.

9Rocky Mountains

Best methods & materials: Log (lodgepole pine is one of the best log species), timber frame, straw bale in the valleys, stone. Steep metal roofs on stout rafters; generous overhangs protect logs from rain and sun.

Climate hazards: Very heavy snow loads (100–300+ psf at high elevations — check the ASCE hazard tool), avalanche paths, wildfire, −30 to −40° cold, strong chinook and downslope winds, intense UV, some earthquakes (Montana, Idaho, Utah, Yellowstone).

Insulation & design: Walls R-25–30+, ceiling R-49–60. Size rafters for local snow (Part 5); don't build below avalanche chutes or at the top of steep forested slopes. Ember-resistant roof and vents, defensible space.

10California

Best methods & materials: Braced wood frame (the most earthquake-tolerant common system), plastered straw bale (performs well in tests), with metal or tile roofs. Earthen buildings only with reinforcement and bond beams (Getty guidelines). Stone and adobe historic in the missions — many collapsed in earthquakes.

Climate hazards: Major earthquakes on the San Andreas and many other faults, wildfire (especially wind-driven fall fires), drought, mudslides after fires, coastal fog and rot, very heavy snow in the Sierra, subterranean and drywood termites in the south.

Insulation & design: Mild climates need moderate insulation (ceiling R-38, walls R-13–21). Anchor bolts, hold-downs and plywood shear walls; strap water heaters and tanks. Ember-resistant vents (⅛ in mesh), metal roof, no wood fences touching the house, 30 m defensible space.

11Northern Mexico

Best methods & materials: Adobe (traditional), rammed earth, stone, and confined masonry (block or brick with concrete tie-columns and beams); palapa and ramada shade structures of palm, carrizo cane and ocotillo; flat roofs on vigas.

Climate hazards: Extreme heat and drought, monsoon flash floods, hurricanes on the Pacific (Baja, Sonora, Sinaloa) and Gulf (Tamaulipas) coasts, earthquakes in Baja California, cold winters in the Chihuahua and Sierra Madre highlands.

Insulation & design: Thick mass walls, small shaded openings, courtyards, light roofs with insulation; cross-ventilation at night. Reinforce heavy walls with bond beams; keep adobe off the ground on stone footings.

12Central & Southern Mexico

Best methods & materials: Confined masonry (as CENAPRED's safe self-build guide shows), bahareque (timber or bamboo frame with woven cane and mud — flexible and earthquake-resistant), bamboo (otate) and wood; adobe with seismic reinforcement in the highlands; palm-thatch palapas on the coasts.

Climate hazards: Large earthquakes (Mexico City 1985 and 2017), active volcanoes (Popocatépetl, Colima), hurricanes on both coasts, intense rainy-season downpours, landslides and floods, termites in the lowlands.

Insulation & design: Highlands: mass walls and passive solar for cool nights. Lowlands: raised, shaded, ventilated, light buildings. Follow CENAPRED: firm ground away from slopes and riverbanks, stone/concrete footings, castillos and dalas tying every wall, light well-tied roofs.

13Central America & Caribbean

Best methods & materials: Bahareque and bamboo (guadua grows in Central America), raised timber frame, confined masonry; palm thatch is traditional but blows away in hurricanes — use well-strapped metal roofs on hip frames, with thatch only for outbuildings.

Climate hazards: Hurricanes, earthquakes (Guatemala 1976, Haiti 2010), volcanoes, very heavy rain, floods and landslides, salt-air corrosion, intense termites and rot.

Insulation & design: Shade and ventilation, not insulation. Raise floors 0.5–1 m on piers; hip roofs at 4:12–6:12 with short eaves; shutters; continuous ties from roof to foundation. Avoid unreinforced block and adobe; use stainless or galvanized fasteners near the sea.
