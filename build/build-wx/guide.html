Part 1Reading the Sky

With no forecasts, sirens or weather radio, your own eyes and a homemade barometer are the warning system. This guide shows how to read clouds and pressure, then what to do in each disaster North America faces. Everything here can be done by ordinary people with household materials.

1.1 The ten basic clouds

CloudLooks likeWhat it tells you
CirrusWhite, delicate filaments; always ice crystalsFair now; watch whether it thickens [3]
CirrostratusThin milky veil; makes a halo around sun or moonThickening high cloud; often the first sign of a wet spell [3]
AltostratusGray sheet; sun looks as if through frosted glass; no haloMay drop light rain or virga [3]
NimbostratusDark gray layer formed from thickening altostratus; blots out the sunSteady rain or snow; base lowers as it continues [3]
CumulusPuffy, flat dark base, cauliflower topForm in the morning on clear days and fade by evening [3]
CumulonimbusHuge tower, often an anvil topThunder, lightning, heavy rain, hail and tornadoes [3]
StratusUniform gray layerDrizzle or snow grains; blue sky when it breaks up [3]

1.2 Cloud sequences that mean rain or snow

  • Watch the trend, not one look. High wisps that spread into a veil, then lower to a gray sheet that hides the sun, are the classic run-up to steady precipitation [3].
  • Cumulus that keep growing taller by early afternoon can become cumulonimbus — the cloud that makes hail and tornadoes [3].
  • Combine sky signs with your barometer (Part 2): falling pressure plus thickening cloud is a much stronger warning than either alone [1,17].

1.3 Thunderstorm clouds and lightning distance

If you can hear thunder, you are in rangeThunder can be heard only about 10 miles from the strike, so hearing it means you are within striking distance and should get to a safe place immediately [4]. Count from flash to thunder and divide by 5 for miles — but do your counting from shelter [4].

Part 2Air Pressure and a Homemade Barometer

Low pressure usually means stormy weather and high pressure usually means clear weather [17]. A barometer that only shows whether pressure is rising or falling is enough to forecast a day ahead.

2.1 Build a can or jar barometer

  1. Cover the top of a small metal can or glass jar with plastic wrap or a balloon with the narrow end cut off. Pull it tight and airtight and hold it with a rubber band [1,2].
  2. Lay a drinking straw flat with one end at the center of the cover and tape it down; the rest sticks out past the rim as a pointer [1].
  3. Stand a card behind the straw (tape it to the can or to a wall) and mark where the tip points [1,2].
  4. Keep it out of the sun and away from heaters, in a spot where it won't be bumped [17].
  5. Read it at the same time each day; mark the highest reading HIGH and the lowest LOW over the first week [1,2].
It shows change, not numbersA homemade barometer shows relative changes only — it cannot match official readings [17]. Temperature also affects the air in the jar, which is why it must stay out of direct sun and away from the stove.

2.2 Keeping a weather log

BarometerSkyLikely next 12–24 h
RisingClearing, cumulus fading at eveningFair, calmer [3,17]
SteadyNo changeMore of the same
Falling slowlyHigh veil, halo, then gray sheetSteady rain or snow coming [3,17]
Falling fastTowering cumulus / dark massesStorms, strong wind — prepare now [3,17]
Log itEach morning and evening write: date, time, straw position, sky, wind direction, and what weather followed. After a few weeks your own log becomes the best local forecast tool you have [1,17].

Part 3Storms: Thunder, Tornado, Hurricane

3.1 Lightning

  • When thunder roars, go indoors: get into a building or a hard-roofed car [5].
  • Indoors, stay off anything wired to an outlet and avoid running water (plumbing conducts lightning) [5].
  • Trim or remove trees that could fall on your home [5].
  • Thunderstorms also bring flash floods, hail and damaging winds [5].

3.2 Tornadoes

  • Best: a safe room or storm shelter. Next best: a small, interior, windowless room or basement on the lowest level of a sturdy building [6].
  • Cover your head and neck with your arms and put furniture and blankets around or on top of you [6].
  • In a car there is no safe option, only less dangerous ones: if the tornado is far away and visible, drive at right angles to its path; if caught, stay belted with your head below the windows, or lie in ground clearly lower than the road [6].
  • Never shelter under a bridge or overpass — little protection from debris [6].
  • Afterwards: stay clear of downed lines; wear thick-soled shoes, long pants and work gloves for clean-up [6].
Build a shelter nowWith no warning sirens, a dug-in storm cellar or a reinforced interior room is the single best tornado investment. See the Bunkers and Concrete guides for building methods.

3.3 Hurricanes and storm surge

  • Atlantic season (including the Gulf and Caribbean): June 1 – November 30. Eastern Pacific: May 15 – November 30 [7,8].
  • Storm surge has historically been the leading cause of hurricane deaths in the US [7]. If you live on low coastal ground, plan your route to high ground well before the season.
  • Hurricanes also bring flooding rain, wind, rip currents and tornadoes far inland [7].
  • Before the storm: clear drains and gutters, bring in loose outdoor items, and shutter windows (plywood works) [7].

Part 4Water and Fire

4.1 Floods and flash floods

Turn around, don't drownJust six inches of moving water can knock you down, and one foot of moving water can sweep a vehicle away. Stay off bridges over fast-moving water [9,10].
  • Floods are the most common disaster in the United States; flash floods can come without warning [9].
  • Move to higher ground or a higher floor [9]. Where it rains, it can flood — flooding does not follow lines on a map [9].
  • In canyons and dry washes, a storm you cannot see upstream can send a wall of water — camp and shelter well above the channel [9,10].

4.2 Wildfire

  • Make a fire-resistant zone at least 30 feet around your home, free of leaves, debris and flammable material [11].
  • Have an outdoor water source with a hose that reaches every part of the property [11].
  • Pick one room you can seal from outside air for smoky days; close all doors and windows [11].
  • Know two ways out and leave early — wildfires spread quickly [11].
Signs of fire weatherHot, dry, windy days after a dry spell are the dangerous ones. Smoke columns, ash fall and an orange sky upwind mean prepare to leave.

Part 5Shaking Ground and the Sea

5.1 Earthquakes: Drop, Cover, Hold On

  • Indoors: stay inside — do not run outside, and avoid doorways [12]. In bed: lie face down and cover head and neck with a pillow [12].
  • No table nearby: crawl next to an interior wall away from windows, stay on your knees and bend over [12].
  • In a car: pull over, stop and set the parking brake. Outside: move to open ground away from buildings, trees, streetlights and power lines [12].
  • Prepare now: secure bookcases, water heaters and heavy items to the wall; keep heavy and breakable things on low shelves [12].

5.2 Tsunamis

Natural warning = leave nowA strong coastal earthquake, a loud roar from the ocean, or the sea suddenly rising or draining to show the sea floor are tsunami signs. Do not wait — go to high ground as far inland as possible [13].
  • Pick refuges 100 feet or more above sea level, or at least one mile inland [13].
  • Tsunami waves can be 10–100 feet high and come in a series — the first wave is often not the largest, so stay away until the sea has been calm a long time [13].
  • If you are in a boat, go out to sea [13].

Part 6Cold and Heat

6.1 Blizzards, wind chill, frostbite and hypothermia

ConditionSignsWhat to do
FrostbiteNumbness; white or grayish-yellow skin; firm or waxy skinGet to a warm room; soak in warm water or warm with body heat. Do not massage or use a heating pad [14]
HypothermiaShivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness; below 95 °F is an emergencyWarm room; warm the center of the body first — chest, neck, head, groin; keep dry and wrapped in blankets including head and neck [14]
  • Limit time outside, dress in layers, and avoid overexertion shoveling snow — winter storms raise the risk of heart attacks [14].
  • Insulate, caulk and weather-strip; keep pipes from freezing [14].
  • Generators and fuel-burning heaters outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors and attached garages; carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless [14].

6.2 Extreme heat

IllnessSignsWhat to do
Heat crampsMuscle pains or spasms in stomach, arms or legsCool place; sip water [16]
Heat exhaustionHeavy sweating, paleness, cramps, weakness, fast or weak pulse, dizziness, headache, fainting, nausea, vomitingCool place; remove extra clothing; sip water [16]
Heat strokeBody temperature above 103 °F; red, hot, dry skin with no sweat; rapid strong pulse; confusion or unconsciousnessLife-threatening. Move to shade, remove outer clothing, cold wet cloths on head, neck, armpits and groin or soak clothing with cool water; raise the feet [16]
  • Extreme heat causes more deaths each year than any other weather hazard [16].
  • Fans give a false sense of comfort and do not lower body temperature in extreme heat [16]. Shade windows with drapes, shades or reflectors [16].

Part 7Hazards Region by Region

1Arctic & Subarctic
Extreme cold and blizzards dominate: watch the wind chill and the signs of frostbite and hypothermia [14,15]. Southern Alaska is earthquake and tsunami country — strong shaking near the coast means go to high ground at once [12,13].
2Pacific NW & BC
Earthquakes and tsunamis on the Cascadia coast and in BC: practise Drop, Cover, Hold On and know the way to ground 100 ft up or a mile inland [12,13]. Long winter rains bring floods and landslides [9]; summer wildfire smoke [11].
3Prairies & Great Plains
Tornado Alley: spring and early-summer supercells [6]. Large hail, blizzards with near-zero visibility, and flash floods in creek beds [9,14]. Watch for towering cumulonimbus building in the west in the afternoon [3].
4Great Lakes & Midwest
Tornadoes, severe thunderstorms and river flooding in spring; lake-effect snow and bitter wind chill in winter [6,9,15].
5Northeast & Atlantic Canada
Nor'easters and blizzards, coastal flooding, and late-season hurricanes reaching New England and Atlantic Canada (Atlantic season June 1 – November 30) [7,8,14].
6Southeast & Gulf
Hurricanes and storm surge on the Gulf and Atlantic coasts — storm surge is historically the leading cause of US hurricane deaths [7]. Tornadoes in spring and with landfalling hurricanes [6,7]. Summer heat and humidity [16].
7Florida
Hurricane exposure on every coast, June through November [7,8]; daily summer lightning storms — when thunder roars, go indoors [4,5]. Low ground floods fast [9].
8Desert SW & Great Basin
Extreme heat: heat stroke means body temperature above 103 °F and hot, dry skin [16]. Summer monsoon storms send flash floods down dry washes [9,10]. Wildfire and dust storms [11].
9Rocky Mountains
Afternoon mountain thunderstorms — get off peaks and ridges early [4,5]; blizzards and avalanches in winter [14]; wildfire in dry summers [11].
10California
Earthquakes anywhere in the state [12]; tsunamis on the coast [13]; wildfire season with dry offshore winds [11]; winter storms bring flooding and mudslides [9].
11Northern Mexico
Heat, drought and summer monsoon flash floods inland [9,16]; eastern Pacific hurricanes (May 15 – November 30) strike Baja California and the Pacific coast [7,8].
12Central & Southern Mexico
Strong earthquakes along the Pacific coast and in central Mexico [12]; tsunamis on the Pacific coast [13]; hurricanes from both the Pacific and the Gulf [8]. Rainy-season floods and landslides [9].
13Central America & Caribbean
Hurricanes from June to November (Caribbean is part of the Atlantic basin) [8]; earthquakes and tsunamis along the Pacific and Caribbean margins [12,13]; tropical downpours cause flash floods [9].
