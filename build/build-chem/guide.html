Part 1Lab Fundamentals

This guide exists for one reason: to keep chemical know-how alive. If the chain of factories, power grids and trained chemists were ever broken, the information here is the ladder back up — how to make, purify and prove the acids, alkalis, solvents and medicines that a modern world takes for granted, starting from salt, stone, wood, air and water. It goes much deeper than the Medicine guide’s summaries: full processes, the principles underneath them, balanced equations, rough yields, equipment, temperatures and catalysts, step-by-step procedures, troubleshooting, and how each thing was first done at small scale before modern plants existed.

This is a reference, not a licence to improviseEvery process here involves corrosive acids, toxic or flammable chemicals, high heat, pressure or electricity. A book can preserve knowledge; it cannot make the chemistry safe. Work only with real ventilation, eye and skin protection, the correct materials, and a tested procedure. Scale down, go slow, and never run a reaction you do not understand. Nothing here is medical advice, and no product described should be dosed into a person or animal.

Deliberate exclusions, stated plainly: this guide contains no explosives formulations or weaponisation, no chemical-weapon agents, no controlled-substance or opioid synthesis, and no dosing experiments. A few chemicals here are dual-use (for example nitric acid and saltpetre); they are covered only for legitimate medical, laboratory, agricultural and industrial purposes, and the text says so where they appear.

1.1 Three tiers of sophistication

Throughout, methods are sorted into three tiers so you can match ambition to resources. Tier A is improvised: fire, clay, scavenged containers, plant materials. Tier B is a real but modest lab: borosilicate glass, a balance, a thermometer, a furnace with forced air. Tier C is industrial: chambers, towers, catalysts, pressure vessels and electrolysis cells. Every tier is genuine chemistry; the higher tiers buy you purity, yield and safety, not magic.

1.2 Glassware and improvised equivalents

Borosilicate glass is the chemist’s best friend: it resists thermal shock and most acids [26]. Where you have none, improvise — but know each substitute’s limits (metal reacts with acids; earthenware can be glazed for acid work; copper tolerates heat but not strong oxidisers).

1.3 Heating, cooling and stirring

  • Gentle, even heat comes from a water bath (up to 100°C), a sand bath (to ~300°C) or an oil bath. Direct flame cracks glass and chars organics — avoid it for distillations.
  • Cooling: an ice bath (0°C), ice + salt (down to −20°C), or simply a long water-cooled condenser for refluxing vapours.
  • Stirring: hand-swirling, a glass rod, or a boiling chip to prevent bumping. A simple magnetic stirrer is a Tier-B luxury.
  • Reflux — boiling a mixture under a vertical condenser so vapour returns — lets you hold a reaction at its boiling point for hours without losing solvent.

The classic laboratory flame is the Bunsen burner (Robert Bunsen, 1855): gas enters at the base, mixes with air drawn through an adjustable collar, and burns at the top of a barrel. Open the air and you get a hot, clean, pale-blue flame; close it and you get a cooler, luminous yellow flame. Bunsen famously never patented his burner, so the closest patent record is the later improved burners in Part 9.

1.4 Measuring mass, volume and temperature

Quantitative chemistry needs numbers. A balance gives mass (and so moles, via molar mass); a graduated cylinder gives volume; a thermometer tracks reaction and vapour temperature. Keep a set of known masses to check a home-made balance. Stoichiometry — the mole ratios in a balanced equation — tells you how much of each reactant to weigh and how much product to expect.

Moles make it simplemoles = mass ÷ molar mass. A balanced equation gives the ratio of moles reacting. Convert back to grams or millilitres (using density) at the end. Always identify the limiting reagent — the one that runs out first sets the maximum yield.

1.5 pH and plant indicators

You do not need a meter to measure acidity. Red-cabbage juice is a broad-range indicator: red in acid, purple near neutral, green to yellow in base, because its anthocyanin pigment changes structure with pH [9,10]. Litmus comes from boiling certain lichens; turmeric turns red above about pH 8.

1.6 Filtration and drying agents

Separate solids from liquids by gravity filtration (folded paper or cloth in a funnel) or, faster, vacuum filtration (a Buchner funnel over a flask under suction). Dry solids in warm air or a desiccator; dry liquids with a drying agent that grabs water without reacting — anhydrous calcium chloride, magnesium sulfate, or quicklime for ethanol [20].

1.7 Safety culture, PPE and first aid

Minimum protectionSplash goggles, acid-resistant gloves, an apron, closed shoes, tied-back hair. Keep a running water supply within reach. Never eat, drink or work alone with dangerous reagents. Label everything — an unlabelled bottle is a future accident.
Chemical first aid (while someone fetches help, where help exists)
  • Acid or base on skin: flush with copious running water for 15+ minutes; remove soaked clothing.
  • Acid or base in eye: flush eye with water for 15+ minutes, lids held open.
  • Fire: smother small fires; sand bucket for burning metals; never water on burning oil or reactive metals.
  • Inhaled fumes: get to fresh air at once; chlorine and nitrogen oxides can injure lungs hours later — rest and watch.
  • Swallowed chemical: do NOT induce vomiting for acids/alkalis/petroleum; rinse mouth, sip water if conscious.

1.8 Ventilation and waste disposal

Many reactions release toxic or flammable gas (chlorine, HCl fumes, nitrogen oxides, hydrogen). Work outdoors or under a fume hood — a hooded, vented bench that pulls fumes away from your face (see the 1906 chemical-desk patent in Part 9). Neutralise acid waste with lime or soda before disposal; never pour incompatible wastes together; keep oxidisers away from fuels.

Part 2Separation & Purification

Making a chemical is half the battle; purifying it and proving it is pure is the other half. Separation exploits differences in boiling point, solubility, or affinity for a surface.

2.1 Simple distillation

Distillation separates liquids by boiling point: heat the mixture, the lower-boiling component vaporises first, a cooled condenser turns it back to liquid, and you collect it in a receiver [47]. Put the thermometer bulb at the side-arm so it reads the vapour temperature — that tells you what is actually coming over.

Prevent bumping and over-pressureAdd boiling chips, never fill a flask more than two-thirds, and never heat a closed system — always leave a vent path or you build dangerous pressure.

2.2 Fractional distillation

Close-boiling liquids need a fractionating column packed with glass beads, rings or coils. Each repeated evaporation-condensation step inside the column is a theoretical plate; more plates give a sharper separation. Reaching above ~95% purity for a 20–30°C boiling-point gap needs at least three theoretical plates [1,3].

2.3 Steam distillation

Heat-sensitive, water-insoluble substances (essential oils, some organics) can be carried over by steam below 100°C. Because each immiscible component exerts its own vapour pressure, the mixture boils when the sum of pressures reaches atmospheric — so it distils cool enough not to char [2]. The distillate separates into an oil layer and a water layer.

2.4 Vacuum distillation

Lowering the pressure lowers the boiling point, so high-boiling or heat-sensitive liquids can be distilled without decomposing [3]. A hand or rotary vacuum pump, a strong (round-bottom) flask that will not implode, and a cold trap are the essentials. See the vacuum-dehydration patent in Part 9.

2.5 Recrystallisation

The standard way to purify a solid: dissolve the crude in the minimum volume of hot solvent, hot-filter off insoluble dirt, then cool slowly. Pure crystals grow while soluble impurities stay dissolved in the mother liquor [5]. Choose a solvent that dissolves the product hot but little cold.

2.6 Extraction and partition

Shake a mixture with two immiscible solvents (say water and an organic solvent) and each solute distributes between them according to its partition coefficient K [4]. Several small extractions remove more than one large one. A separatory funnel (or improvised jar and tap) lets you draw off the lower layer.

2.7 Chromatography and melting point

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) spots a tiny sample on a coated plate and runs it in solvent; each compound travels a characteristic fraction of the way (its Rf), so one spot means one compound [6]. Column chromatography scales the same idea to separate grams [7]. A sharp melting point at the literature value is a classic identity-and-purity test for a solid.

2.8 Titration and standard solutions

To measure how concentrated an acid or base is, titrate it against a standard solution of accurately known concentration, using an indicator to spot the endpoint [8]. A primary standard (pure, stable, weighable — e.g. dried sodium carbonate) lets you standardise your working solutions. From the balanced equation and the volume used, back-calculate the unknown.

Part 3Raw Materials from the Land

Every reagent traces back to something you can dig, burn, evaporate or grow. This part covers the feedstocks; Part 4 turns them into acids and alkalis.

3.1 Salt and brine

Sodium chloride — from seawater evaporation, salt springs or rock salt — is the master feedstock. It gives chlorine, hydrogen and caustic soda by electrolysis (Part 4.5), hydrochloric acid, and sodium carbonate via the Solvay process. Purify crude salt by dissolving, filtering and recrystallising.

3.2 Limestone and the lime cycle

Heating limestone (calcium carbonate) drives off carbon dioxide to give quicklime (CaO); adding water gives slaked lime (calcium hydroxide); exposure to air slowly carbonates it back. This lime cycle underpins mortar, water treatment, and dozens of reactions.

CaCO₃ →(heat ~900°C) CaO + CO₂↑ ; CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂
The lime kiln is one of the oldest chemical reactors — see the 1882 hydraulic-lime patent in Part 9.

3.3 Wood ash and potash

Leaching hardwood ashes with water and boiling down the liquor gives potash (impure potassium carbonate); further purification by calcining gives whiter pearl ash [13,14]. Potash is a base, a source of potassium salts, and (with lime) a way to make caustic potash (KOH) for soft soap.

3.4 Saltpetre beds

Potassium nitrate forms where nitrogen-rich organic matter decays with air, lime and ash present: soil bacteria oxidise ammonia to nitrate, which is leached out and crystallised [11,12].

Dual-use note. Saltpetre (potassium nitrate) is dual-use. This guide covers it only for its legitimate roles — nitrogen fertiliser, a traditional food curing salt, a source of nitrate for making nitric acid for laboratory and medical glassware cleaning, and heat-treatment salts. It gives no explosive formulations or ratios.

3.5 Sulfur sources

Sulfur comes from volcanic deposits, from roasting metal sulfide ores (pyrite), or historically by melting and separating native sulfur from its gangue [45]. It is the feedstock for sulfuric acid (Part 4.1).

3.6 Charcoal, coal and destructive distillation

Heating wood or coal without air (destructive distillation) leaves a carbon-rich solid and drives off valuable volatiles. From wood you get charcoal plus pyroligneous acid — the first industrial source of acetic acid, methanol and acetone [15,16]. From coal you get coke plus coal tar, the historic source of phenol, benzene and aniline dye feedstocks.

3.7 Kelp and iodine

Burning seaweed to ash, leaching it, and treating the liquor liberates iodine — historically the main source before mineral brines [48]. Iodine makes antiseptic tincture and Lugol’s solution (Part 7.4).

Part 4Acids & Alkalis in Depth

The bulk acids and bases are the hardest things for a small operation to make well, and the most important. Here is how each was first made and how it is made now.

4.1 Sulfuric acid — lead-chamber and contact

Sulfuric acid is the keystone industrial chemical. Two routes matter. The older lead-chamber process burns sulfur to SO₂, then oxidises it to acid inside lead-lined chambers using nitrogen oxides as a recyclable catalyst, giving roughly 65% acid [28,45]. The modern contact process passes dried SO₂ over a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst at 400–450°C to make SO₃, which is absorbed into existing acid (to avoid a choking mist) and then diluted to 98% [27,34].

4.2 Nitric acid

Two routes again. The historical lab route gently distils saltpetre with sulfuric acid and condenses the nitric acid vapour — glass or stoneware only, because hot nitric attacks most metals [46]. The industrial Ostwald process catalytically oxidises ammonia over platinum to NO, then NO₂, then absorbs it in water [29].

Dual-use note. Nitric acid is dual-use. It is covered here only for legitimate uses: making laboratory reagents, cleaning and passivating glassware and stainless steel, metal etching and refining, and the medicinal/analytical nitrates. No energetic-material preparations are given.

4.3 Hydrochloric and acetic acids

Hydrochloric acid: historically a by-product of the Leblanc salt-cake step; simply made by treating salt with sulfuric acid and dissolving the hydrogen chloride gas in water (NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl↑). Acetic acid: from oxidising ethanol (vinegar fermentation) or from the pyroligneous acid of wood distillation; concentrate by distillation. Acetic anhydride (needed for acetylation) is made industrially from acetic acid via ketene; it is the harder reagent to make and often the limiting step for aspirin.

2 CH₃COOH → (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O
Acetic anhydride from acetic acid (via ketene, CH₂=C=O, industrially). Store away from moisture — it hydrolyses back to acetic acid.

4.4 Phosphoric acid

Made by treating phosphate rock (or bone ash, calcium phosphate) with sulfuric acid (the wet process), or by burning phosphorus to P₂O₅ and hydrating it (the thermal process, higher purity). A mild acid catalyst and a buffering agent in medicine.

4.5 Soda, caustic soda and potash

Sodium carbonate (soda ash) came first from the wasteful Leblanc process and then the elegant Solvay process, which recycles its ammonia [30,31,32]. Caustic soda (NaOH) and chlorine are made together by electrolysis of brine (the chloralkali process); a diaphragm or membrane keeps the chlorine and caustic from recombining [30]. Potash gives caustic potash (KOH) by the lime causticising reaction.

4.6 Ammonia

Historically recovered from the destructive distillation of coal (ammoniacal liquor) and from Solvay recovery. The Haber-Bosch process fixes atmospheric nitrogen: N₂ + 3 H₂ ⇌ 2 NH₃ over an iron catalyst at high pressure and ~400–450°C [33]. It is the hardest process here to reproduce at small scale because of the pressures involved — a true Tier-C undertaking.

N₂ + 3 H₂ ⇌ 2 NH₃ (ΔH < 0)
High pressure favours the smaller gas volume (fewer moles) of ammonia; moderate temperature balances rate against the unfavourable equilibrium at high heat (Le Chatelier) [34].

Part 5Gases & Elements

5.1 Chlorine, hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide

The four workhorse gases are each made by a simple reaction and collected by a method suited to their density and solubility [17,18].

Chlorine and hydrogenChlorine gas damages the lungs at low concentrations — never breathe it. Hydrogen forms explosive mixtures with air — no flames nearby, and test small. Oxygen supercharges any fire.

Hydrogen and oxygen can also be made by electrolysis of water (acidified so it conducts): direct current splits water, hydrogen collecting at the cathode in twice the volume of oxygen at the anode. The same apparatus, run on brine, is the chloralkali cell of Part 4.5; run to make hydrogen peroxide, it is the antiseptic route of the Medicine guide.

5.2 Iodine, bromine and phosphorus

Iodine from kelp or brine (Part 3.7), purified by sublimation (it goes straight from solid to vapour and back). Bromine from seawater bitterns by chlorine displacement — extremely corrosive and toxic, handle only with full protection. Phosphorus is made by strongly heating phosphate rock with carbon and silica in a furnace (a demanding Tier-C reduction); covered here for matches, phosphoric acid and phosphates only.

5.3 Collection, storage and hazards

Store reactive gases dissolved or as stable salts rather than as compressed gas where possible. Keep oxidisers (chlorine, nitric acid, saltpetre) physically apart from fuels and organics. Vent every reaction that generates gas.

Part 6Organic Chemistry Foundations

6.1 Ethanol: fermentation to anhydrous

Yeast ferments sugars to ethanol and CO₂; distillation concentrates it, but only to 95.6% — the ethanol-water azeotrope, a constant-boiling mixture that ordinary distillation cannot pass [19,21]. To reach anhydrous (>99%) ethanol, dry the 95% spirit over quicklime (CaO) or a 3Å molecular sieve, or break the azeotrope with an entrainer [20]. Ethanol is solvent, antiseptic, fuel, and a reagent for esters and ether.

6.2 The named reactions

A handful of reaction types build almost every small-molecule medicine. Learn these and the syntheses in Part 7 become variations on a theme.

6.3 Mechanisms behind the medicines

  • Esterification (acid + alcohol) and its reverse, hydrolysis/saponification (ester + base → soap + alcohol), explain aspirin, benzocaine and soap [24].
  • Nitration then reduction converts an aromatic ring to an amine (ArH → ArNO₂ → ArNH₂) — the gateway to sulfa drugs, local anaesthetics and dyes.
  • Acetylation caps a reactive –NH₂ or –OH, used to make paracetamol and aspirin [36,37].
  • Diazotisation and azo coupling turn an aromatic amine into a brilliant dye, and historically into sulfa-drug intermediates [22,23].

Part 7Worked Syntheses — Step-by-Step with Troubleshooting

This whole part is Tier B/C: it assumes a working lab with ventilation, glassware, heat, cooling and reagents. Its purpose is knowledge retention — these are the published routes by which humanity first made its most important medicines, explained in more detail than the Medicine guide. Nothing here is a kitchen project, and no product should ever be dosed into a person or animal.

Before any synthesisEvery route below uses corrosive acids, toxic or flammable reagents, and heat. Work with proper ventilation, protection, the right glassware, and the original published procedure in front of you. Verify purity (Part 7.5) before you even call something by the drug’s name.

7.1 Salicylic acid and aspirin

Salicylic acid is made industrially by the Kolbe-Schmitt reaction: dry sodium phenoxide is heated under CO₂ pressure (~100°C, several atmospheres), carboxylating the ring; acidifying gives salicylic acid [35]. At small scale it can also be obtained by hydrolysing methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen) with sodium hydroxide, then acidifying.

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is then made by acetylating salicylic acid with acetic anhydride and a trace of acid catalyst [36].

  1. Weigh salicylic acid; add acetic anhydride (slight excess) and a few drops of phosphoric or sulfuric acid as catalyst.
  2. Warm to about 85°C in a water bath for 10–15 minutes, swirling. Do not overheat.
  3. Cool; add cold water to destroy excess anhydride and crash out crystals.
  4. Vacuum-filter, wash with a little cold water.
  5. Recrystallise from hot water (or water/ethanol), cool slowly, filter and dry.
  6. Verify: a negative iron(III) chloride test (no purple = no leftover phenol) and a sharp melting point of 135–136°C.
Troubleshooting aspirinNo crystals? Scratch the flask, cool further, or add a seed crystal. Purple with FeCl₃? Unreacted salicylic acid remains — recrystallise again. Low, wide melting point? Impure or wet — dry fully and re-purify. Yield from 10 g salicylic acid is about 13 g in theory; expect noticeably less after purification.

7.2 Paracetamol and the local anaesthetics

Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is made by acetylating 4-aminophenol with acetic anhydride [37]. Benzocaine is the ethyl ester of 4-aminobenzoic acid, made by acid-catalysed esterification [38]. Procaine and lidocaine build on the same amine-and-amide/ester chemistry [39]; lidocaine is an amide (more stable) rather than an ester.

Dual-use note. Local anaesthetics are covered for knowledge-retention only. They are dangerous if injected impure or mis-dosed; this guide gives no dosing. Several steps genuinely need an industrial chemical supply behind them.

7.3 Sulfanilamide

The first sulfa drug. The classic route protects aniline as acetanilide, chlorosulfonates it to a sulfonyl chloride, treats that with ammonia to the sulfonamide, then removes the protecting group by hydrolysis [40]. It is a clean teaching example of protect → functionalise → deprotect, and of why a sequence matters.

7.4 Ether, Dakin’s solution, iodine tincture and saline

  • Diethyl ether: heat ethanol with excess sulfuric acid near 140°C — the acid dehydrates two ethanol molecules into ether and water [41]. Extremely flammable, forms explosive peroxides on storage; no flames, store dark and cool with an antioxidant.
  • Dakin’s solution: dilute sodium hypochlorite buffered with sodium bicarbonate to near-neutral pH for wound irrigation [42]. Hypochlorite comes from passing chlorine into cold sodium hydroxide.
  • Iodine tincture / Lugol’s: dissolve iodine (and potassium iodide, which greatly increases solubility) in water or alcohol [43].
  • Normal saline: 9 g of purified sodium chloride per litre of sterile (boiled) water, 0.9% [44].
EtherDiethyl ether is one of the most dangerous things in this guide to make and store: its vapour is heavier than air, travels along benches to a distant flame, and it forms shock-sensitive peroxides over time. If you make it, make little, use it quickly, and never store it long.

7.5 Verifying purity

For every solid product: recrystallise, then confirm identity and purity by a sharp melting point at the literature value and, where possible, a single TLC spot against a reference [5,6]. For a reagent solution, titrate to find its real concentration [8]. Never trust a synthesis by appearance alone.

Part 8Scaling Up & Energy

8.1 Furnaces and heat sources

Chemistry is limited by the temperature you can reach and hold. A wood fire struggles past 600°C; forced air from a bellows or blower turns the same charcoal into a 1300°C forge; coke and electric kilns go higher still. Forced draft is the single biggest and cheapest upgrade.

8.2 Pressure vessels and their dangers

Pressure killsA ruptured pressure vessel is a bomb regardless of its contents. Any closed, heated system (autoclaves, Haber-type reactors, Kolbe-Schmitt carbonation) must have a tested relief valve or burst disc, a pressure gauge, and a barrier between it and people. Never improvise a pressure vessel from unrated pipe or containers.

Steam sterilisers (autoclaves) and small reactors are the common pressure vessels in a lab. The sterilising-apparatus patents in Part 9 show proper chamber, jacket and valve design — study them before building anything that holds pressure.

The humble pressure cooker is the Tier-A autoclave: trapped steam raises the boiling point to about 121°C at ~15 psi, hot enough to sterilise instruments and media — exactly the method the Medicine guide gives for sterilising surgical tools. A weighted regulator holds the pressure and a safety valve prevents a burst. Part 9 collects real autoclave and pressure-cooker patents.

8.4 The lab notebook and teaching the next generation

Knowledge survives only if it is written down and taught. Keep a bound lab notebook: date, aim, exact quantities, observations, yields, what went wrong. Record failures — they are the most valuable entries. Teach by apprenticeship: let a learner weigh, titrate and distil under supervision until the technique is in their hands, not just on paper. A single surviving notebook of tested procedures is worth more than any one batch of product.
