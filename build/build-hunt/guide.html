Part 1Before You Start

1.1 The law, and when this guide applies

Legal noticeIn normal times, hunting, trapping, snaring and fishing are regulated by state, provincial and federal law: seasons, licenses, legal methods and protected species. Many devices in this guide (snares, deadfalls, gill nets, fish traps) are illegal for most people in most places. This guide describes them for a true survival emergency, when lawful options no longer exist. Until then, follow your local regulations.

The U.S. Army survival manual notes that several well-placed traps can catch much more game than a person with a rifle is likely to shoot, and that fish are usually more plentiful than mammals and can be caught silently [1]. Traps work for you while you do other jobs. Check them at least daily — and always approach carefully: every mammal can bite, and a cornered or trapped animal will fight [1].

1.2 Reading sign: where to set traps

There is no trap that catches everything. Find out what lives in the area and set traps for that species [1]. Look for [1]:

  • runs and trails, tracks, droppings;
  • chewed or rubbed plants;
  • nesting or roosting sites, feeding and watering places.

A trail is used by several species and is distinct; a run is smaller and used by one species. Set traps between bedding, feeding and watering areas [1].

Don't spook the animal
  • Build trap parts away from the site, then carry them in [1].
  • Do not use freshly cut live wood: its sap smells like danger [1].
  • Mask your scent: mud from rotting vegetation, smoke from a fire, or fluid from the gall and urine bladders of earlier kills. Not human urine [1].
  • Or let a trap weather a few days untouched before setting it [1].
  • Bait with something the animal knows but cannot easily find nearby; salt works well. Scatter a little around the trap [1].

Part 2Snares

Traps and snares crush, choke, hang or entangle. The power is the animal's own struggle, gravity, or a bent sapling; the heart of every trap is the trigger [1].

2.1 Simple snare and drag noose

  • Simple snare: a noose over a trail or den hole, tied to a firmly planted stake. Hold it open with twigs or grass. Make the loop big enough to pass over the head. It usually does not kill, and cord may loosen and slip off — wire is best [1].
  • Drag noose: two forked sticks either side of a run, a crossbar laid loosely across, the noose hung from it above head height [1].

2.2 Twitch-up snare

A twitch-up is a supple hardwood sapling, stripped of branches, bent over and held by a trigger [1].

  1. Bend the sapling and mark the spot on the trail under it [1].
  2. Drive the long leg of one forked stick into the ground there; cut its short leg parallel to the ground [1].
  3. Tie the long leg of the second forked stick to cord from the sapling; cut its short leg to hook under the first one [1].
  4. Hang a noose over the trail; bend the sapling and engage the two short legs [1].

2.3 Treadle snare

For small game on a trail: a shallow hole covered by thin sticks resting on a horizontal bar held between two forked stakes. A noose powered by a twitch-up or hanging weight is spread over the sticks, and a short trigger stick held by line tension keeps the bar in place. When the animal steps on a cover stick, the bar drops, the trigger releases, and the noose catches the foot [1]. The disturbed trail makes animals wary, so channel them in [1].

2.4 Squirrel pole and noosing wand

  • Squirrel pole: lean a long pole against a tree with lots of squirrel activity. Fix several wire nooses along it. Keep the end nooses 45 cm from the ends so a squirrel cannot get its feet on something solid and chew the wire. Several squirrels can be caught on one pole [1].
  • Noosing wand: a pole with a wire or stiff-cord slip noose at the tip, slipped over a roosting bird or held over a den hole from a hiding place [1]. It takes patience.

Part 3Deadfalls & Other Traps

3.1 Figure-4 deadfall

Three notched sticks hold up a weight heavy enough to kill or stun the animal immediately. Practice beforehand — the trigger needs close fits and exact angles [1].

Deadfalls can injure youSet the weight last, and keep hands and feet out from under it. A trigger sensitive enough to catch game can drop on your fingers. Never set large deadfalls where people or pets may walk.

3.2 Paiute deadfall

  1. Tie one end of a cord to the lower end of the diagonal stick; tie the other end to a 5 cm catch stick [1].
  2. Bring the cord halfway round the upright with the catch stick at a right angle [1].
  3. Set the bait stick with one end against the weight (or a peg) and the other against the catch stick [1].
  4. When the animal disturbs the bait stick, the catch stick releases and the weight drops [1].

3.3 Bottle trap

For mice and voles: a hole 30–45 cm deep, wider at the bottom than the small top, covered by bark propped 2.5–5 cm up on stones. Rodents hide under the cover and fall in; the sloping walls stop them climbing out. Snakes hide in these holes — check carefully [1].

Part 4Fishing Without a Store

Fish feed heavily before a storm and seldom after one when water is muddy. They rest in eddies near rocks, in deep pools, under overhanging brush and around logs and weeds. Light attracts fish at night [1].

4.1 Hooks and gorges

Make hooks from pins, needles, wire, small nails, or wood, bone, thorns, flint or shell [1]. A wooden hook: a hardwood shank about 2.5 cm long and 6 mm thick, notched at one end, with a point of bone, wire or nail lashed in. A gorge: a small shaft sharp at both ends, tied at the middle and baited lengthwise; the fish swallows it [1].

4.2 Stakeout lines

Drive two supple saplings into the bottom with tops just under the surface, tie a line between them just below the surface, and hang two short baited lines that cannot wrap around the poles, each other, or slide along the main line [1].

4.3 Making and setting a gill net

  1. Tie a strong top line between two trees. Hang many lines from it by doubling them over (girth hitches or prusik knots). The net length and mesh size set how many lines and how far apart [1].
  2. Row 1: tie lines 2 and 3 together with an overhand knot, then 4 and 5, 6 and 7, to the end [1].
  3. Row 2: tie 1 to 2, 3 to 4, and so on — always one line from a pair to one from the next pair [1].
  4. Tie a guideline across the trees as a gauge and move it down after each row so mesh stays even [1].
  5. Thread a strong line along the bottom edge. Set the net across part of a stream or along a shore where fish move [1].

4.4 Fish traps and tidal traps

  • Fish basket: sticks lashed with vines into a funnel; close the top leaving a hole just big enough for fish to swim in [1].
  • Tidal trap: schools come in with the tide and move along the shore. Pick the spot at high tide, build at low tide: a low stone wall angled out from shore, or block rock pools and sandbar ditches as the tide falls [1].

4.5 Spearing fish

In waist-deep water with many large fish: sharpen a long straight sapling, or split the end, wedge it apart, and sharpen both prongs. Ease the point toward the fish, then push suddenly to pin it to the bottom. Hold it down and grab the fish by hand — do not lift it on the spear. Allow for refraction: fish are not quite where they appear [1].

4.6 Fish that can hurt you

  • There are no poisonous freshwater fish, but catfish spines cause painful punctures that quickly become infected [1].
  • Cook all freshwater fish to kill parasites; also cook saltwater fish caught near reefs or freshwater outlets [1].
  • Some saltwater fish have poisonous flesh, seasonally or always — FM 21-76 lists porcupine fish, triggerfish, cowfish, thorn fish, oilfish, red snapper, jack and puffer; barracuda may carry ciguatera [1].
  • Spoiled fish: sunken eyes, odd smell or color, dents that stay when pressed, slimy body. Cooking does not make spoiled fish safe [1].

Part 5Field Dressing & Butchering

5.1 Field dressing big game

  1. Wear disposable gloves to reduce disease exposure [2,3]. Clean the knife often between cuts [2].
  2. Lay the animal on its back, front legs raised, hind legs spread, propped with rocks or sticks [2].
  3. Cut around the anus to free the bung; tie it off so feces cannot touch the meat [2].
  4. Cut along the midline from breastbone to genitals, lifting skin and muscle together. Do not cut the paunch or intestines [2].
  5. Cut the diaphragm free from the ribs; cut the windpipe and gullet at the base of the throat [2].
  6. Pull out lungs, heart and entrails. Bag the heart and liver and keep them cold [2].
  7. Remove dirt, hair, feces and bloodshot meat; wipe the cavity dry [2,3].
Do not eat this animalIf the organs smell offensive, or there is greenish discharge, black blood or blood clots in the muscle, do not eat meat from the carcass [2,3]. Do not eat animals that looked sick or acted strangely, or that you found dead [6].

Inspect the heart and liver for worms or parasites. A healthy liver is smooth, wet and deep red or purple; discard a diseased-looking liver — the muscle meat may still be usable [1].

5.2 Cooling the carcass

  • Above 40 °F, harmful bacteria grow quickly; aim to chill the carcass surface to 40 °F or lower within 24 hours [3]. Cornell: cool quickly to 35–40 °F [2].
  • Prop the cavity open with a clean stick or hang the carcass so air circulates. A carcass left on the ground holds heat [2,4].
  • In warm weather, pack the cavity with ice or clean snow sealed in plastic bags [2,3]. Quartering and wrapping in cheesecloth helps; avoid tarps and canvas, which hold heat [4].
  • Keep it in the shade. Leave the hide on during transport to keep the meat clean [2].
  • Age only at 40 °F or below, for 2–3 days at most [2].
  • Use food-grade bags or buckets for cut meat — not dark trash bags [3].

5.3 Skinning and cutting up

  • Slit the hide from throat to tail, cutting around the sex organs. Slip the blade under the skin, edge up, so you cut only hide and keep hair off the meat [1].
  • Cut along each leg to the body cut, pull the hide away, cutting connective tissue as needed; remove head and feet [1].
  • Front legs have no joint to the body — cut through the muscle. Cut hindquarters off at the ball-and-socket hip joint [1].
  • Remove the tenderloins along each side of the spine. Break ribs first, then cut through the breaks — easier on the knife [1].
  • Use the heart, liver, kidneys, tongue and bones (for broth) [1]. Preserve extra meat by canning, drying or smoking — see the Food Preservation guide.

5.4 Small game, birds, snakes and fish

  • Small mammals: cut the hide around the middle, put two fingers under each side and pull both halves off [1]. Wear gloves for rabbits and hares (tularemia, 6.1) [5].
  • Birds: pluck or skin, remove entrails (save heart, liver, and the craw of seed-eaters), cut off feet [1]. Remove entrails and crop soon after killing and do not pile warm birds together [4]. Boil scavenger birds at least 20 minutes [1].
  • Snakes: cut off the head and bury it; slit the skin 15–20 cm, peel it back and pull it off; remove entrails; boil or roast in sections [1].
  • Fish: clean as soon as possible; cut out gills and the blood vessels along the spine; gut fish over 10 cm; scale or skin. Boiling with skin on saves the most food value [1].

Part 6Game Diseases & Safe Handling

6.1 Tularemia

A potentially serious bacterial illness. Rabbits, hares and rodents are especially susceptible and often die in large numbers in outbreaks. People catch it from tick and deer-fly bites, skin contact with infected animals, contaminated water, or breathing contaminated dust. It occurs in every U.S. state except Hawaii. Prevent it with insect repellent, gloves when handling sick or dead animals, and by not mowing over dead animals [5]. Most cases respond to antibiotics [5].

6.2 Chronic wasting disease (CWD)

A fatal prion disease of deer, elk and moose, reported in wild deer in 36 U.S. states and in Canada. It has never been reported in people, but scientists cannot rule it out; prions stay in soil for years [6]. CDC advice where CWD occurs [6]:

  • Do not shoot, handle or eat animals that look sick or act strangely, or eat meat from animals found dead.
  • Wear latex or rubber gloves when field dressing.
  • Avoid handling internal organs, especially the brain and spinal cord.
  • Use separate knives and tools — not your kitchen ones.
  • If an animal tests positive, do not eat it.

6.3 Trichinellosis

Caused by worm larvae in raw or undercooked meat of bear, wild boar, wildcat, fox, wolf, seal or walrus (and pork) [7]. Even tasting raw meat while preparing it is a risk. Homemade jerky and sausage from these meats caused many past cases. Curing, drying, smoking or microwaving alone does not reliably kill the worms, and freezing may not kill the species found in wild game [7]. Cook to safe temperatures with a thermometer [7].

6.4 Brucellosis

Bacteria carried by bison, elk, caribou, moose and wild hogs (and livestock). Hunters catch it from undercooked meat, raw milk, or body fluids in the eyes, nose or mouth while dressing animals [8]. Wear rubber gloves, goggles, an apron and a mask when handling tissues; cook meat thoroughly; do not drink raw milk [8].

6.5 Cooking temperatures

Game birds165 °F [4]
Whole cuts, steaks, roasts (Cornell)145 °F [2]

Bear, boar and other trichinella carriersCook these to well done, check with a thermometer, and never taste them raw [7]. Without a thermometer, cook until no pink remains and juices run clear, then cook longer.

Part 7Game & Fish Region by Region

Common game in each region and the special hazards to watch for. Learn local species now, while field guides and wildlife offices are available.

1Arctic & Subarctic
Caribou, moose, hares, ptarmigan, waterfowl, seals and fish. Never eat polar bear or bearded seal liver — toxic levels of vitamin A [1]. Bear, seal and walrus meat can carry trichinella — cook thoroughly; freezing may not kill wild-game species [7]. Caribou and moose can carry brucellosis [8]. Cold helps cooling but frozen-in-the-field carcasses are hard to butcher: dress and quarter quickly.
2Pacific NW & BC
Deer, elk, black bear, rabbits, grouse and huge salmon runs — fish traps and nets (Part 4) can feed families. Gut and bleed fish fast; it spoils quickly [1].
3Prairies & Great Plains
Pronghorn, deer, rabbits, prairie dogs, upland birds and waterfowl; bison where present. CWD has been reported in wild deer in 36 U.S. states and in Canada — follow 6.2 [6]. Rabbits and rodents die in tularemia outbreaks [5]: never pick up a dead or sick one bare-handed.
4Great Lakes & Midwest
White-tailed deer, turkey, squirrels, rabbits, waterfowl and lake fish. Squirrel poles (2.4) work well in hardwood forests. Follow the CWD precautions in 6.2 [6].
5Northeast & Atlantic Canada
White-tailed deer, moose, black bear, snowshoe hare, grouse, and coastal and freshwater fish. Cook bear meat thoroughly (trichinella) [7].
6Southeast & Gulf
Deer, feral hogs, squirrels, rabbits, turkey, catfish and other river fish. Wild hogs carry brucellosis — wear gloves and eye protection when butchering [8]. Watch catfish spines [1]. Warm weather: cool carcasses within hours (5.2) [3].
7Florida
Deer, feral hogs, waterfowl, abundant fish. Some reef fish (barracuda and others) can carry ciguatera toxin; FM 21-76 lists several poisonous saltwater species [1]. Heat spoils game fast — dress immediately and pack with ice if any exists [3].
8Desert SW & Great Basin
Mule deer, rabbits, quail, javelina, bighorn. Water sources concentrate game — set snares on trails to water [1]. Check bottle traps carefully: snakes hide in them [1]. Rabbits can carry tularemia [5].
9Rocky Mountains
Elk, mule deer, moose, bighorn, grouse, trout. Follow the CWD precautions in 6.2 [6]. Elk and moose can carry brucellosis [8]; wear gloves.
10California
Deer, wild pigs, rabbits, quail, coastal fish and shellfish. See the Food guide for shellfish (paralytic shellfish poisoning) warnings.
11Northern Mexico
Deer, javelina, rabbits, quail, and coastal fish in the Gulf of California and Gulf of Mexico. Heat makes fast cooling critical [3].
12Central & Southern Mexico
Deer, rabbits, iguanas, birds, abundant coastal and lake fish. Reptiles are good food but should be cooked [1].
13Central America & Caribbean
Fish and shellfish, iguanas, agouti and birds. Reef fish can carry ciguatera; do not eat barracuda raw [1]. Saltwater fish caught near reefs or river mouths should be cooked [1].
