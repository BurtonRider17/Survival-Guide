Part 1Why Food Spoils — and Botulism

Preserving food means stopping the bacteria, yeasts and molds that rot it. Every method in this guide does that by taking away something they need: heat (canning), water (drying, salting), warmth (cold storage), or by adding acid (fermenting, pickling). Follow tested directions exactly — the times here are from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning and university Extension publications.

1.1 Botulism: the deadly one

The bacterium Clostridium botulinum can make a deadly toxin inside canned food. Its spores are on most fresh food and are harmless in air. They grow and make toxin in 3 to 4 days when they have all of these [1]:

  • a moist, low-acid food;
  • a temperature between 40 °F and 120 °F;
  • less than 2 percent oxygen — exactly the inside of a sealed jar.
You cannot see, smell or taste botulinum toxinEven a small taste of food containing the toxin can be deadly. Never taste food to check whether it is safe. If in doubt, throw it out [6]. Home-canned vegetables are the most common cause of botulism outbreaks in the United States [6].

1.2 Acid foods and low-acid foods

Botulinum spores are very hard to kill at boiling temperature. Low-acid foods must be heated to 240–250 °F, which only a pressure canner at 10–15 pounds can reach [1]. Boiling low-acid food long enough to make it safe would take 7 to 11 hours [1].

Tomatoes need added acidSome tomatoes are slightly above pH 4.6. USDA: add 2 tablespoons bottled lemon juice or ½ teaspoon citric acid per quart (1 tablespoon or ¼ teaspoon per pint) [3]. Four tablespoons of 5% vinegar per quart also works but changes the flavor [3].

1.3 Spotting spoiled jars and getting rid of them

Check every jar before opening [1,6]:

Throw it out if…
  • the lid is loose, bulging or not curved down (not sealed)
  • the jar leaks, or has streaks of dried food down the outside
  • liquid or foam spurts out when you open it
  • you see rising bubbles, cloudiness or unnatural color
  • it smells wrong, or has mold (white, blue, black or green) on the food or lid

Spoiled low-acid food (including tomatoes) may show little or no sign. Treat any suspect jar as containing botulinum toxin [1]:

  1. Still sealed: put the jar in a heavy garbage bag, close it, and put it in the trash or bury it in a landfill pit [1].
  2. Unsealed, open or leaking: wearing rubber or heavy plastic gloves, lay the jars and lids on their sides in a big pot, cover with at least 1 inch of water, put a lid on, bring to a boil and boil 30 minutes. Cool, then throw out jars, lids and food [1].
  3. Clean up: spray or wet anything the food touched with 1 part household bleach (5–6%) to 5 parts water; leave 30 minutes; wipe up with paper towels and bag them; apply the bleach solution again for 30 minutes, then rinse and wash everything [1].
Boil home-canned food before eatingCDC: boil home-canned tomatoes, foods containing them, all low-acid home-canned foods and all home-fermented Alaska Native foods before eating — 10 minutes below 1,000 ft, plus 1 minute for each extra 1,000 ft [6]. Heat destroys the toxin; it does not make a bulging or foul jar safe.

Part 2Canning

2.1 Jars, lids and headspace

  • Use Mason-type threaded home-canning jars with two-piece self-sealing lids. Jars can be reused many times; USDA calls for new flat lids each time [1]. Unused lids work well for at least 5 years from manufacture [1] — stockpile flat lids; they are the part you cannot make.
  • Not recommended: wire-bail jars with glass caps, zinc caps, and jars whose mouths do not take two-piece canning lids [1].
  • Not recommended methods: open-kettle canning, ovens, microwaves, dishwashers, canning powders [1], and electric multi-cookers, even with a “canning” button [6].

2.2 Boiling-water canning step by step

  1. Fill the canner halfway with clean water. Preheat to 140 °F for raw-packed food or 180 °F for hot-packed food [1].
  2. Fill jars, leave the right headspace, wipe rims with a clean damp cloth, put on lids and bands.
  3. Lower jars upright on the rack. Add boiling water until it is at least 1 inch over the jars (2 inches if processing more than 30 minutes) [1].
  4. Cover, heat on high until the water boils vigorously, then start the timer [1].
  5. Keep it boiling the whole time. If the boil stops, bring it back and start the timing over [1]. Add boiling water to keep the level up.
  6. When time is up, turn off the heat, take off the lid, wait 5 minutes, then lift jars out [1].
  7. Set jars on a towel 1 inch apart and leave them undisturbed 12 to 24 hours [1].
Sterilizing jarsJams, jellies and pickles processed less than 10 minutes go into sterile jars: boil empty jars upright, covered by 1 inch of water, for 10 minutes below 1,000 ft, plus 1 minute per extra 1,000 ft [1].

2.3 Pressure canning step by step

  1. Put 2–3 inches of hot water in the canner (more if a recipe says). Load filled jars on the rack. Fasten the lid [1].
  2. Leave the weight off the vent (or open the petcock). Heat on high until steam flows freely [1].
  3. Let steam flow for 10 minutes, then put the weight on or close the petcock. Pressure builds in 3–5 minutes [1].
  4. Start timing when the dial reaches the right pressure, or when the weighted gauge jiggles or rocks as the maker describes [1].
  5. Adjust heat to hold steady pressure at or a little above the target. If pressure drops below the target, bring it back up and start the full time over [1].
  6. Turn off the heat and let the canner cool down by itself to zero. Never force-cool with water [1].
  7. When depressurized, remove the weight, wait 10 minutes, then open the lid tilted away from your face [1]. Cool jars 12–24 hours on a towel [1].
Keep the canner accurateCheck dial gauges every year. A gauge reading up to 2 pounds off can be corrected for; replace gauges off by more than 2 pounds [1]. A weighted gauge needs no accuracy check [1]. Keep gaskets clean and un-nicked, and never scratch or pick at the safety fuse [1]. With no testing station, keep a spare weighted gauge and gasket for your model. CDC: use a canner that holds at least four 1-quart jars upright [6].

2.4 Altitude adjustments

Water boils at lower temperatures as you go up, so sea-level times can leave food unsafe from 1,000 feet up. Use more time (boiling-water) or more pressure (pressure canner) for your altitude [1]. With no way to look it up, use a topographic map or road signs, and round up.

2.6 Testing seals and storing jars

  • After 12–24 hours, take off the bands and test each lid: press the center (it must not spring up), tap with a spoon (a ringing sound means sealed; a dull sound means not), or look across it (it should curve down) [1].
  • Unsealed jar: reprocess within 24 hours with a new lid, the same time; or refrigerate and eat within several days [1].
  • Wash, label and date jars; store without bands in a clean, cool, dark, dry place — best at 50–70 °F, never above 95 °F, not near stoves, pipes, attics or sunlight [1,6].
  • Can only what you will use in about 1 year [6].
  • Freezing does not spoil jars unless they unseal; if they might freeze, wrap them in newspaper and blankets in heavy boxes [1].

Part 3Drying

3.1 Sun drying fruit

Fruit is high in sugar and acid, so it can be dried outdoors when the weather is right. Vegetables (except vine-dried beans) and meat should not be dried outdoors — dry them indoors near a stove or in an oven where heat can be controlled [8].

  • Needs hot, dry, breezy days: at least 85 °F, humidity below 60%; it takes several days [8].
  • Cover or bring fruit inside at night — cool night air adds moisture back [8].

3.2 Jerky

  • Pork and wild game can carry the trichinella parasite. Before slicing, freeze pieces 6 inches thick or less at 0 °F or below for at least 30 days [7]. (Freezing does not kill bacteria, and freezer power may be gone — then use the heating step and cook thoroughly.)
  • If gut contents touched the meat when the animal was shot or dressed, do not make jerky from it; cook it thoroughly instead [7].
  • Slice no thicker than ¼ inch and trim all fat — fat goes rancid [7].
  • Heat to 160 °F: boil strips in the marinade 5 minutes before drying, or heat the dried strips in an oven afterward [7].
  • Dry at 140 °F until a test piece cracks but does not break when bent — 10 to 24 hours if not pre-heated [7].

3.3 Conditioning and storing dried food

  • Cool completely first; warm food sweats and molds [9].
  • Condition fruit (about 20% moisture): pack loosely in closed jars for 7–10 days, shaking daily; if moisture appears, dry it more [9].
  • Vegetables should be brittle (about 10% moisture) and need no conditioning [9].
  • Pack tightly in clean, dry, insect-proof containers, in amounts you will use at once [9].
  • Most dried fruit keeps about 1 year at 60 °F, 6 months at 80 °F; vegetables about half as long [9]. Check often; re-dry damp food; throw out moldy food [9].

Part 4Salting, Curing & Smoking

Salt pulls water out of meat and fish so microbes cannot grow; smoke adds flavor and some protection. Temperature control is the key to doing it safely.

4.1 Dry-curing a ham (Virginia Extension method)

  1. Chill the carcass below 40 °F before cutting, and keep the ham below 40 °F until curing [13].
  2. Mix the cure for every 100 lb of fresh meat: 8 lb salt, 2 lb sugar, 2 oz saltpeter or sodium nitrite. Salt alone is acceptable [13]. Divide into two equal parts.
  3. Day 1: rub the first half into all lean surfaces; cover skin and fat; pack the exposed shank end and the area around the aitchbone (hip bone) well to prevent bone sour [13].
  4. Day 7: apply the second half [13].
  5. Keep hams on draining shelves at 36–40 °F (check with a thermometer); avoid pine and other fragrant woods [13].
  6. Cure 1½ days per pound of ham (or 7 days per inch of cushion depth) [13].
  7. Wash off the cure, then hold at 50–60 °F for about 14 days to equalize. Expect 8–10% shrink [13].
Cure only in cold weatherVirginia Extension: early December is the best time to start curing under ambient conditions [13]. Without refrigeration, you need steady 36–40 °F weather — warm spells spoil hams.

4.2 Brining and smoking fish

  • Brine: 1 cup non-iodized salt to 7 cups water salts 2–3 lb of fish in about 1 hour; herring about 30 minutes; 2–3 inch salmon chunks about 2 hours. Start testing at 15 minutes per ½ inch of thickness. Do not let pieces overlap [12].
  • Rinse and air-dry on a greased rack in a cool place until a shiny, tacky pellicle forms (at least 1 hour) [12].
  • Smoke, then make sure the thickest piece reaches at least 150 °F (preferably 160 °F) and stays there 30 minutes. Small smokers may not get hot enough — finish in an oven [12].
  • Keep smoked fish at 38 °F or less [12]. Without refrigeration, eat it soon, or pressure-can fish (2.5).

Part 5Fermenting

5.1 Sauerkraut

  1. Use firm, fresh cabbage; start within 24–48 hours of harvest [10].
  2. Work 5 lb at a time: remove outer leaves and cores, shred about as thick as a quarter [10].
  3. Add 3 tablespoons canning or pickling salt per 5 lb, mix with clean hands, pack firmly until juice is drawn out [10].
  4. If juice does not cover the cabbage, add boiled, cooled brine (1½ tablespoons salt per quart water) [10].
  5. Weight it down and cover with a towel (5.2). Ferment at 70–75 °F for 3–4 weeks; at 60–65 °F it takes 5–6 weeks; below 60 °F it may not ferment; above 75 °F it may go soft [10].
  6. If you used jars as weights, check 2–3 times a week and remove any scum [10].
  7. Keep finished kraut covered and cold for months, or can it in a boiling-water canner (2.5) [10].

5.2 Crocks, covers and weights

  • Use stone crocks or food-grade plastic or glass. Non-food-grade buckets only if lined with a food-grade bag — never garbage bags [11].
  • Keep vegetables 1–2 inches under brine with a plate weighted by water-filled jars, or a sealed food bag holding 3 quarts of water with 4½ tablespoons salt [11].
  • Wash the crock, plate and jars in hot soapy water and rinse with very hot water first [11].

Part 6Root Cellars & Cold Storage

Stored vegetables are alive and breathing; cold slows them down. Most keep best between 32 and 40 °F with high humidity [15]. Choose late-maturing, good-keeping varieties and harvest as late as you can before a hard frost [15].

6.1 The four storage groups

  • Keep fruit and vegetables apart. Ripening apples and pears give off ethylene gas, which makes potatoes sprout early [14].
  • Strong-smelling cabbage and turnips taint other food — store them away from the house or wrap them in newspaper [14,15].
  • Potatoes and onions need curing in a warm place first; onions and squash want dry, airy storage and mold in damp air [15].
  • Pack root crops in moist sand, sawdust or peat, or in perforated plastic bags [15]. Do not let anything freeze [14].

6.2 Running a root cellar

  • A dirt floor keeps humidity up; on concrete or wood, set out pans of water. A small, full cellar holds humidity better than a big, empty one [15].
  • Warm air rises — the top is warmer. Put thermometers at several heights [15].
  • Use the vents to let in cold outside air and let out damp, warm air; close them when it is warmer outside than inside, and in deep cold so nothing freezes.
  • No cellar? An unheated back hall, enclosed porch, shed, or a walled-off room in the coldest (north/east) corner of a basement can work [16].

Part 7Long-Term Grain & Dry Storage

7.1 Packing grain, rice and beans

  • Temperature: a steady 40–60 °F is best; USU cites USDA that shelf life halves for every 10.8 °F rise [17].
  • Moisture: food must be dry — about 10% moisture or less [17].
  • Oxygen: remove it with oxygen absorbers, dry ice (carbon dioxide) or nitrogen [17].
  • Containers: #10 cans, sealable food buckets, lined drums, foil (Mylar-type) pouches, PETE bottles, or glass jars kept dark [17,18]. Rodents chew through plastic bags [18].
  • Insects: freeze infested food at 0 °F for 3–4 days to kill insects, larvae and eggs [17]. Wheat need not be oxygen-free unless insects are present [18].
Never seal moist food without oxygenBotulism poisoning may result if moist products are stored in packaging that removes oxygen. Only dry foods (about 10% moisture or less) go in with oxygen absorbers [17,19].

7.2 How long it lasts

“Life-sustaining” shelf-life estimates for properly packaged dry foods stored cool, from BYU research [19]:

FoodYears
Wheat, white rice, corn, sugar30+
Pinto beans, rolled oats, pasta, potato flakes, apple slices30
Non-fat powdered milk, dehydrated carrots20

USU: rice in oxygen-free containers keeps up to 10 years at a constant 70 °F and up to 30 years in cooler storage [18]. A #10 can holds about 5.7 lb of rice [18]. Store wheat whole and grind it as needed — whole grain keeps far longer than flour [17].

Part 8Region by Region

1Arctic & Subarctic
Cold is your freezer for much of the year, and root cellars work very well in cold soils [15] — but they still need a heat source (as little as a 100-watt bulb in the UAF guide) so produce does not freeze [15]. Smoked and dried fish are staples; follow the brine-and-heat rules in 4.2 [12]. CDC: people in Alaska with botulism usually got it from traditional fermented foods — boil home-fermented Alaska Native foods before eating [6].
2Pacific NW & BC
Damp, mild air makes sun drying unreliable — dry indoors near a stove. Salmon runs make smoking and pressure-canning fish the big jobs; PNW 238 was written for this region [12]. Cool basements and garages make good cold rooms [16].
3Prairies & Great Plains
Dry air and sun dry fruit well; hard winters make root cellars and cold storage easy. Prairie grain (wheat) stores for decades when kept dry and cool [17,18]. Protect stores from mice in grain country.
4Great Lakes & Midwest
Big gardens and orchards: pressure-can vegetables, ferment cabbage in fall (kraut needs 70–75 °F, so start before the house gets cold) [10], and use the root cellar for potatoes, carrots and apples [14].
5Northeast & Atlantic Canada
Classic root-cellar country; apples and cabbage store well cold and moist [14,16]. Fish and game can be salted, smoked or pressure canned. Plan curing for winter: Virginia Extension says early December is the best time to start curing hams under ambient conditions [13].
6Southeast & Gulf
Heat and humidity are the enemies. NCHFP notes high humidity in the South is a problem for outdoor drying [8]; dry indoors. Keep canned food below 95 °F [1] — store it in the coolest, lowest room. Dry-curing needs 36–40 °F [13], which only happens in winter here.
7Florida
Hot and humid all year: canning and well-sealed dry storage beat drying and curing. Jars stored above 95 °F lose quality and may spoil [1]. Few places stay cold enough for a root cellar; grow crops you can harvest year-round instead.
8Desert SW & Great Basin
Ideal sun-drying weather (hot, dry, breezy) [8]. Much of the region is above 1,000 ft: add canning time or pressure for altitude (2.4) [1]. Daytime heat can spoil stored food — dig storage into the ground or use north-facing rooms.
9Rocky Mountains
High altitude everywhere: use the altitude columns in every table, and run weighted-gauge canners at 15 lb above 1,000 ft [1]. Cold, dry winters suit root cellars and long-term grain storage.
10California
The Central Valley is one of the best sun-drying places on earth — it produces much of the world's raisins [8]. Mountain areas need altitude adjustments [1].
11Northern Mexico
Hot and dry; outdoor fruit drying works well [8]. Heat in storage shortens shelf life — USU: shelf life halves for every 10.8 °F rise [17]. Much of the plateau is high: adjust canning for altitude [1].
12Central & Southern Mexico
Highland cities (Mexico City is far above 6,000 ft) need the highest altitude column in every canning table [1,2,3]; lowlands are hot and humid. Dried and salted foods are traditional; keep them in sealed jars against moisture [9].
13Central America & Caribbean
Hot and humid: dried foods reabsorb moisture fast, so pack into small sealed jars you can use all at once [9]. Store canned goods in the coolest spot (under 95 °F) [1]. Salt-and-heat rules for smoked fish apply in any climate [12].
