Part 1Climate, Zones and Timing

A garden is the most reliable long-term food source once stores are gone. This guide covers growing vegetables from your own saved seed, building soil with compost and ash, and stretching the season with frames and covers — with no stores, labs or hired help. See the Food guide for calorie crops and the Preservation guide for keeping the harvest.

1.1 Hardiness zones in the US and Canada

  • The USDA map divides the US and Puerto Rico into 13 zones, each a 10 °F band of average coldest winter temperature, split into 5 °F half-zones ‘a’ and ‘b’ [1].
  • Canada has its own plant hardiness zone map from Natural Resources Canada, updated in 2025; many areas shifted half a zone or more — Yellowknife moved from zone 0b to 1b [2].
  • Zones matter most for perennials (fruit trees, berries, asparagus). For annual vegetables, the frost dates and soil temperature matter more [3].
  • Before the internet goes: write your zone and your average last spring and first fall frost dates on the inside cover of this guide.

Part 2Soil

2.1 The jar test: what is your soil?

  • Sandy soil drains fast and dries out: add compost every year to hold water.
  • Clay soil holds water and packs hard: add compost, never work it wet, and use raised beds.
  • Whatever the texture, organic matter (compost) is the fix — see Part 3. Block-style beds work best on soils improved to 4–5% organic matter [3].

2.2 Raising pH with wood ash

  • Wood ash contains calcium, potassium, magnesium and trace elements, and acts like lime to raise pH on acid soils [9].
  • Limit: no more than 10 lb per 100 sq ft per year. Sift it, apply about two weeks before planting, on moist soil, raked in lightly [9].
  • Do not use on soils above pH 7.0, on blueberries, rhododendrons or azaleas, or at seeding time (salts burn seedlings) [9].
  • Never use ash from trash, cardboard, coal, barbecue charcoal or treated, painted or stained wood [9].
Ash is causticHandle wood ash like lye or bleach: eye protection, gloves, dust mask, closed shoes; never spread on windy days [9]. Too much in one spot can effectively sterilize the soil [9].

2.3 Beds, spacing and walkways

Block or close-row beds 2–4 feet wide, any length, with 2-foot walkways, let you reach the middle without stepping on and compacting the soil [3]. Plants are spaced equally in all directions (e.g. beets 6 inches apart each way) [3].

Part 3Compost

3.1 Building a compost pile

  1. Pick a spot with good drainage, easy to reach all year, with water nearby; sun or shade both work [6].
  2. Start with a 4–6 inch layer of twigs or wood chips for air and drainage [6].
  3. Layer greens and browns like lasagna, using at least two to three times as much brown as green by volume; always bury food scraps under 4–8 inches of browns [6].
  4. Keep it as moist as a wrung-out sponge and turn it from time to time [6].

3.2 Heat, turning and finishing

  • A well-kept backyard pile can reach 130–160 °F, which reduces pathogens and weed seeds [6].
  • Decomposition is fastest between 90 and 140 °F; above 160 °F the pile can kill its own microbes and even risk spontaneous combustion — turn it when it passes 140 °F [7].
  • When it no longer heats after turning and no food scraps show, let it cure at least four weeks. A tended pile is ready in about 3–5 months; untended, about a year [6].

Do not plant the same family in the same spot more than once every three to four years; even changing families every year helps against pests and disease [4,5]. Keep a written map of each year's beds [4].

4.2 Cover crops

Sow a cover crop on beds that would otherwise sit bare, and include cover crops in the rotation — a cover crop counts as its family (for example, peas and clover are legumes) [4,5]. Turn it into the soil before it sets seed.

Part 5Seed Saving

5.1 Open-pollinated vs. hybrid

  • Save seed only from open-pollinated (including heirloom) varieties: their seed grows plants like the parent [10].
  • Seed from hybrids (such as ‘Big Boy’ or ‘Early Girl’ tomatoes) grows, but the plants are an unpredictable mix [10].
  • Easiest: tomatoes, peppers, beans and peas — they self-pollinate [10]. Harder: corn and squash family, which cross-pollinate by wind or insects; carrots and beets need two seasons to make seed [10].
Stockpile the right seed nowHybrid seed packets are useless for a second generation. Buy open-pollinated varieties now (e.g. ‘Brandywine’ or ‘San Marzano’ tomato, ‘Kentucky Wonder’ or ‘Blue Lake’ bean, ‘Little Marvel’ pea) [10].

5.3 Storing seed and cleaning it

  • Store in tightly sealed glass, cool and dry; 32–41 °F is ideal [10].
  • Add a desiccant: silica gel, or 1–2 tablespoons of fresh powdered milk wrapped in cloth or tissue (works about six months) [10].
  • Label with name, variety and date; seed loses germination and vigor with age — plan to use it within a year and keep growing it out [10].
  • To clean suspect seed: 1 part 5.25% bleach to 4 parts water with a few drops of dish soap, 1 minute, then rinse 5 minutes in cool water [10].

Part 6Season Extension

6.1 Row covers and low tunnels

  • Floating row covers keep plants about 5–10 °F warmer and give frost protection down to about 28 °F; they also block insects [11,12].
  • On wire hoops (low tunnels) they give about 4–5 °F of frost protection [13].
  • Secure all edges against wind [12]. Uncover for pollination of crops that need insects, and slit or open tunnels before temperatures inside top 90 °F [12,13].

6.2 Cold frames and hot beds

  • A cold frame is a bottomless box with a glass or clear plastic lid that opens for ventilation — an old window works [12].
  • Face it south to southeast on well-drained soil, with a wall or hedge to the north if possible; sinking it into the ground adds insulation [12,13].
  • Ventilate on sunny days or it overheats; close it early enough each afternoon to hold heat for the night [12,13].
  • Uses: harden off seedlings, start crops weeks early, grow salad greens into winter, store root crops [11,13].
  • A hot bed adds heat from fresh manure buried beneath the root zone [13].

Part 7Growing Region by Region

1Arctic & Subarctic
Very short, cool seasons: rely on hardy crops — peas, spinach, lettuce, radish, turnips, cabbage, potatoes [3] — and on cold frames, row covers and hot beds [11,12,13]. On the Canadian map Yellowknife is now zone 1b [2].
2Pacific NW & BC
Mild, wet winters let hardy crops grow most of the year under row covers [11,13]. Raise beds for drainage; acid soils may benefit from wood ash — test pH first, at most 10 lb per 100 sq ft per year [9].
3Prairies & Great Plains
Hot summers and cold winters; strong drying winds. Plant hardy crops 2–4 weeks before the last frost and very tender crops 2+ weeks after [3]. Windbreaks and mulch help; save seed from plants that thrived in your conditions [10].
4Great Lakes & Midwest
Zones about 3–6 [1]. Classic four-bed rotation fits well [4]; cold frames and low tunnels add weeks at each end [11,12].
5Northeast & Atlantic Canada
Short to moderate seasons and often acidic soils — test before adding ash [9]. Maine's extension recommends cold frames facing south to southeast and row covers to gain time [12].
6Southeast & Gulf
Long, hot summers: cool-season crops lose quality in heat [3], so grow them in fall, winter and early spring and keep summer for beans, corn, squash and tomatoes. Rotation is vital against soil pests and nematodes [4,5].
7Florida
Zones about 9–11 [1]. The main vegetable season is the cooler half of the year; summer heat and rain favor heat-lovers. Very hot soil can stop germination — check the maximum temperatures in the planting table [3].
8Desert SW & Great Basin
Two seasons: cool crops in fall–winter, warm crops in spring before peak heat [3]. Hot soil stops germination of lettuce, peas and spinach above about 70 °F [3]. Shade cloth and mulch save water.
9Rocky Mountains
Short seasons at altitude and frost possible in any month in high valleys — Colorado's extension guide was written for these conditions [3]. Use cold frames and row covers [11,13].
10California
Mediterranean climate: cool crops through the mild, wet winter; warm crops in the dry summer with irrigation [3]. Zones about 8–10 on the coast and much colder in the Sierra [1].
11Northern Mexico
Desert and high plateau: like the Desert Southwest, grow cool crops in the cool months and warm crops in spring and early summer [3]; hot soil limits germination [3].
12Central & Southern Mexico
Altitude, not latitude, sets the season: cool highlands suit hardy crops while lowlands suit heat-lovers [3]. Plant with the rains; save seed from local open-pollinated varieties [10].
13Central America & Caribbean
Frost-free lowlands (zones 10–13 in Puerto Rico [1]); timing follows wet and dry seasons. Heat limits cool-season crops [3]; rotate to break soil pest cycles [5].
