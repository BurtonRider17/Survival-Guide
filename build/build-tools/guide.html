Part 1The Essential Tool Kit

When factories and stores stop, the tools you already own — and the ones you can collect in the first months — are what let you build shelters, fences and water systems, grow food, and repair everything else. Hand tools last for generations if they are good steel and kept sharp, dry and properly handled. Power tools are a bonus while fuel or batteries last; plan as if every job will eventually be done by hand.

1.1 What to stockpile or salvage first

Prioritize tools that are hard to make (files, saws, drill bits, hardened steel), wear out (files, sandpaper, whetstones, saw blades) or are needed for food and water (shovels, hoes, axes). Tools that are easy to make in a forge (hooks, hinges, nails, simple tongs) are a lower priority than the hardened tools that make the forge work possible.

Part 2Tool Care: Sharpening, Rust, Handles & Saws

A dull tool is slow and dangerous: it glances off wood, takes more force, and tires you out. Keeping tools sharp, rust-free and tightly handled is a daily skill in a grid-down world. The Forest Service ax and saw manuals in the sources section are the best free references on this subject — print them.

2.1 Sharpening

Files remove metal fast and are used on softer tools: axes, mauls, shovels, hoes, machetes, mower blades, saw teeth. A file cuts only on the push stroke — lift it on the return or you dull it. Clean clogged teeth with a file card. Push the file into the edge (toward the cutting edge) with the tool clamped, and wear gloves. Whetstones (natural or man-made, oil or water) finish what the file starts and sharpen hardened tools a file cannot cut — knives, chisels, plane irons. Work coarse → fine: coarse (~200–400 grit) for repair, medium (~800–1000) for sharpening, fine (3000+) for polishing. Natural stones: Arkansas novaculite (Ozarks/Ouachitas) is one of the best in the world; fine sandstone, slate and river stones also work.

2.2 Rust prevention and cleaning

Rust is the main enemy of stored tools, especially in humid regions. Extension services give the same basic routine: clean off soil and sap after each use, dry, oil, and store dry and off the floor.

  • Daily: knock off soil, wipe dry, wipe metal with an oily rag (keep one in a can by the door). A bucket of coarse sand moistened with vegetable oil or clean motor oil (not used oil, which is toxic) lets you plunge shovels and hoes in to clean and oil them in one move.
  • Remove rust: wire brush, steel wool, sandpaper, or a block of pumice; for heavy rust soak in vinegar 12–24 hours, scrub, neutralize in baking-soda water, dry and oil immediately.
  • Protective coatings: light machine oil, gun oil, lanolin, paste wax, beeswax/tallow rubbed on warm metal, or boiled linseed oil (dries to a thin film). For long storage, coat in grease or wax and wrap in oiled paper.
  • Storage: hang tools on a wall away from concrete floors (which wick moisture); keep files separated so their teeth do not touch; store saws with a slotted wood guard on the teeth.
Linseed-oil rags can catch fire by themselvesRags soaked in boiled or raw linseed oil heat up as they dry and can ignite spontaneously. Spread them flat outdoors to dry, or put them in a metal can full of water with a lid.

2.3 Handles and rehafting

Best handle woods are tough, shock-resistant hardwoods: hickory (the North American standard), white ash, hop-hornbeam (ironwood), black locust, white oak, sugar maple, persimmon and Osage orange. Choose straight-grained split wood (not sawn across the grain) from the trunk, free of knots. Split a log into quarters with wedges, rough-shape with a hatchet, and let the blank dry slowly for months (indoors, out of the sun; seal the ends with wax, paint or glue) before final shaping. A handle shaved green will shrink and loosen in the head.

  1. Remove the old handle: saw it off just below the head, drill several holes into the wood left in the eye, then drive the plug out through the top (the wide end) with a drift or a large bolt. Never burn out an old handle — heat can draw the temper of the head.
  2. Fit: shave the top of the new handle with a rasp, spokeshave or knife until the head slides on about two-thirds of the way. Smoke the eye with a candle or rub with pencil graphite; tap on, remove, and shave only the shiny/marked high spots. Repeat until the head seats with the handle standing ¼–½ in (6–12 mm) above the top of the eye.
  3. Saw the kerf (slot) for the wedge if the handle lacks one: down the long axis of the eye, two-thirds to three-quarters of the eye depth.
  4. Seat the head: hold the handle vertical, head down but not touching anything, and strike the end of the handle (the knob) with a mallet. The head’s inertia drives it onto the handle.
  5. Wedge: make a dry hardwood wedge as long as the eye and slightly thicker than the kerf. Glue optional. Drive it until it stops moving, then saw off the excess ⅛ in (3 mm) above the head. A steel wedge driven across it at 45° adds grip.
  6. Finish: sand, then soak the wood with boiled linseed oil (several coats over days). Do not varnish or paint the grip — it causes blisters and hides cracks. Soaking a loose head in water only works briefly and crushes wood fibers; re-wedge or rehang instead.
Other handlesHammer and hatchet handles fit the same way. Shovel and hoe handles fit into a socket: taper the end to match, drive in, and fix with a single screw or rivet. Files must have a handle — a bare tang can stab into the palm. Any short piece of drilled hardwood or a corncob works.

2.4 Saw setting and filing

Handsaws need two things to cut well: sharp teeth and enough set (teeth bent alternately left and right) so the kerf is wider than the blade and the saw does not bind. Rip saws cut with the grain using chisel-shaped teeth; crosscut saws cut across the grain using knife-pointed teeth. Clamp the saw in a saw vise (two boards in a bench vise work) with the teeth just above the jaws.

Part 3Primitive & Improvised Tools

Steel tools will be around for a long time after a collapse, but knowing how to make tools from stone, bone, wood and fiber fills gaps — a lost knife on a hunting trip, a need for many scrapers, or cordage for a hundred uses. These skills also make you better at using and repairing modern tools. The U.S. Army survival manual (ATP 3-50.21) covers field-expedient tools in detail.

3.1 Stone tools and knapping basics

Knappable stone breaks with a smooth, shell-like (conchoidal) fracture, like thick glass: flint and chert (found as nodules and layers in limestone and chalk, and as river cobbles), obsidian (volcanic glass — the sharpest edge known, but brittle), chalcedony, jasper, agate, novaculite, and fine-grained quartzite and basalt. Test a candidate stone by striking a thin edge: it should ring, not thud, and the break should be glassy and smooth, not grainy. Where no stone is available, thick bottle glass and porcelain insulators knap well.

TechniqueToolUse
Hard-hammer percussionRounded hammerstone (quartzite cobble)Strike large flakes from a core; rough out blanks (bifaces)

Method: sit with a thick leather pad on your thigh and a leather pad in your palm. Strike near the edge of the stone at a steep angle (less than 90° between the striking surface and the face you want to remove); the flake comes off the face below the blow. Simple sharp flakes are excellent knives and scrapers without any further work. A hand axe or celt (ground stone axe) can be made by pecking a tough stone (basalt, diorite, greenstone) into shape with a hammerstone and grinding it on wet sandstone — slow, but such axes fell trees and do not shatter.

Knapping hazardsFlakes are razor-sharp and fly: wear safety glasses, leather gloves/pads and long pants. Knap outdoors over a tarp — the fine dust from flint and quartzite is crystalline silica, which scars the lungs if breathed regularly; never knap indoors without ventilation and sweep wet, not dry. Many quarry sites (Alibates, Flint Ridge, Obsidian Cliff, Knife River) are protected archaeological sites: do not dig or collect there.

3.2 Bone, antler and shell

Fresh, dense leg bones of deer, elk, cattle or moose are tough and easily shaped. Split them by scoring with a stone flake and snapping, or by sawing; grind to shape on sandstone. Bone makes awls and needles (for leather and nets), fish hooks and gorges, scrapers for hides, chisels and wedges, and punches. Antler is tougher than bone and makes the best knapping billets, pressure flakers, wedges for splitting logs, and tool handles. Soak antler in water for a day to soften it before carving. Shell (clams, conch in the Caribbean) gives scrapers, spoons and blades.

3.3 Wood tools

  • Digging stick: straight hardwood 3–4 ft long, point fire-hardened (char lightly over coals and scrape — repeat; do not burn deeply). The basic gardening tool of many cultures.
  • Mallets and mauls: a section of dense wood (hop-hornbeam, oak, hickory, dogwood, mesquite, burls) with the handle carved from the same piece. Use to drive chisels, froes and wedges.
  • Wedges (gluts): split from dense dry hardwood; drive with a wooden maul to split logs into rails, boards and shingles.
  • Rakes, pitchforks, shovels: forks from a naturally branched sapling (steam- or heat-bend and tie while drying); shovels from split boards with edges protected by sheet-metal straps.
  • Spoons, bowls, trays: carved green from birch, maple, basswood, alder, cherry; hollow by burning with coals and scraping.

3.4 Cordage

Strong cord is made by reverse-wrap twisting: twist a bundle of fibers until it kinks into two strands, then twist each strand in one direction while wrapping them around each other in the opposite direction. Splice in new fiber before a strand runs out. Two-ply cord is much stronger than the same fiber unspun.

3.5 Shaving horse and spring-pole lathe

A shaving horse is a bench with a foot-operated clamp that holds wood while you shape it with a drawknife or spokeshave: tool handles, pegs, shingles, chair legs, rake teeth, bows. A spring-pole lathe turns wood for tool handles, bowls and spindles using a springy sapling and a foot treadle — no electricity. Both can be built from split logs, a few augered holes and pegs.

Part 4Blacksmithing

Blacksmithing turns scrap steel into the things a community constantly needs: hooks, hinges, nails, chains, tools, knives, and repairs for farm equipment. A basic shop needs only a forge (fire + air), an anvil (any heavy hard steel), hammers, tongs, a vise, and water. The Artist-Blacksmith’s Association of North America (ABANA) and its local affiliates across the U.S. and Canada run classes — learning from a smith now is worth more than any book.

Charcoal: expect to burn roughly a 5-gallon bucket per hour of steady forging; make it by the barrel-load (section 4.3). Hard lump charcoal from oak, hickory, maple or mesquite lasts longest; softwood charcoal burns faster but is fine with gentler air.

4.2 Build a brake-drum forge

The brake-drum forge is the classic beginner build: a cast-iron vehicle brake drum is a ready-made firepot that tolerates heat. Truck drums (15–16 in diameter) hold a bigger fire than car drums.

Brake-drum forge parts and steps
  • Brake drum; wash off grease and brake dust with water (wet-wipe — old brakes may contain asbestos; never blow the dust off)
  • Steel table or frame at a height where the fire is about at your wrist with arms hanging; a steel wheel rim or old grill also works
  • 1½–2 in BLACK steel pipe: floor flange, short nipple, tee, 6–8 in nipple down to a threaded cap (ash dump), side pipe 2–3 ft to the blower
  • Grate: ¼ in steel plate with 5–7 holes of ½ in drilled over the drum hole, or a few bolts/rebar pieces across the hole
  • Air: hand-crank blower, a large bellows, an old hair dryer or vacuum on 'blow', or inflatable-toy blower; a gate valve or sliding plate to throttle it
  • Optional: line the drum with a mix of clay and sand (about 50/50) to shape a firepot 4–5 in deep; let it dry slowly
  • Light with paper and kindling over the grate, add fuel around it, start low air. Bank wet coal around the edge (coking ring) and push it in as coke
Never use galvanized pipe or fittings in a forgeGalvanized pipe (dull gray, crystalline “spangled” zinc coating) gives off zinc oxide fumes when hot, causing metal fume fever. Use black iron pipe or remove the coating first (soak in vinegar outdoors until fizzing stops). See Part 7.

4.3 Making charcoal

Charcoal is wood heated without enough air to burn: the water and tars drive off as smoke and gas, leaving nearly pure carbon. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) manuals on simple charcoal making describe pits, earth mounds, metal kilns and retorts. For a household smithy, a double-barrel retort gives the best yield and the cleanest charcoal; a pit needs no equipment.

  • Wood: dry (seasoned 6+ months), split to 1–3 in (2.5–7.5 cm) thick, cut to fit. Dense hardwoods make denser, longer-burning charcoal. Do not use painted, treated or plywood scraps — toxic fumes and contaminants.
  • Signs of progress: white steam (drying) → thick yellow-brown smoke (tar, the wood is cooking) → thin bluish smoke and a jet of flame at the gas pipe → smoke stops: finished. A 30-gallon inner drum takes about 3–6 hours.
  • Cooling: seal every opening with earth or clay and let it cool completely, overnight or longer. Opening hot charcoal to air can make it burst into flame.
  • Good charcoal is black, light, rings when tapped, and breaks with a shiny surface. Brown, soft ends (“brands”) are undercooked — put them back in next batch. Sort into egg-to-walnut size for the forge.
Charcoal making makes carbon monoxide and starts wildfiresRun retorts and pits outdoors, downwind of homes, on bare mineral soil with a 10 ft (3 m) cleared circle, and never on windy, dry days. Keep water and a shovel at hand. Store finished charcoal in metal containers — fresh charcoal can self-heat and ignite if piled while still warm or damp. Never burn charcoal indoors or in a closed tent or garage to cook or heat.

4.5 Anvils, hammers and tongs

An anvil only has to be heavy, hard and solidly mounted. The rule of thumb is that the anvil should weigh about 50 times the hammer: 100+ lb (45 kg) is ideal for general work, but much less works for small items. Set the face at about knuckle height with your arms hanging, on a hardwood stump or a steel stand filled with sand. Test with a ball bearing dropped from 10 in (25 cm): it should rebound most of the way; a low, dead bounce means a soft or cracked face.

Hammers: start with a 2–2½ lb (0.9–1.1 kg) cross-peen; the peen moves metal in one direction (drawing). A ball-peen and a 4 lb hammer cover most other work. 4.6 Steel basics and identifying salvaged steel

The single most important number about steel is its carbon content. Below about 0.3% carbon (mild steel, rebar, bolts, angle iron, nails) steel cannot be meaningfully hardened by quenching: it is easy to forge and weld and good for hooks, hinges, brackets and tongs. Above about 0.5–0.6% (springs, files, blades, tool steels) steel becomes very hard when heated and quenched: it is needed for anything that cuts or is struck — knives, chisels, punches, axe bits. Cast iron (2–4% carbon: engine blocks, old pans, brake drums, pipe fittings, stove parts) cannot be forged at all: it crumbles under the hammer.

Other quick tests: (1) Magnet — all steels and iron are magnetic; most stainless (300-series) and non-ferrous metals are not. 4.9 Forge welding

Forge welding joins two pieces of iron or steel by heating both to near-white heat and hammering them together. It was the only way to join metal for thousands of years and is still the way to put a high-carbon steel bit into a mild-steel axe or make chain.

  1. Clean, deep fire: well-coked coal or charcoal, at least 4–6 in (10–15 cm) of glowing fuel over the air blast. A dirty fire with clinker will not weld.
  2. Scarf the ends: upset slightly, then hammer each to a short taper so they overlap with a convex face (the weld closes from the middle outward, pushing out slag).
  3. Flux: heat to bright red, sprinkle with borax (laundry borax works; it can be dehydrated in a pan first), or for wrought iron clean fine sand. Flux melts and keeps air off the surface.
  4. Return to the fire and bring to welding heat: light yellow to near white, with the flux bubbling like water and the first few sparks just starting. Heat both pieces evenly; turn them.
  5. Move fast: pull both, tap off scale, lay them together on the anvil and strike lightly first to set the weld, then harder. Reheat to welding heat and work the weld again to consolidate it.
Easiest first weldFold a bar of mild steel back on itself (a “faggot” weld) or close a chain link — the pieces are already held together, so you can concentrate on heat. Wrought iron and mild steel weld most easily; high-carbon steel welds at a lower heat (yellow) and burns if overheated.

4.10 Heat treating: annealing, hardening, quenching, tempering

Heat treatment only works on steel with enough carbon (about 0.5% or more). The metallurgy: heated past its critical temperature (≈ the non-magnetic point, ~1,400–1,500°F / 760–815°C for common carbon steels) steel changes to a structure called austenite that dissolves carbon; quenching fast traps the carbon in a very hard, brittle structure called martensite; tempering then trades a little hardness for toughness.

Part 6Woodworking for Construction

With axes, saws, augers and chisels, you can build houses, barns, fences and furniture without nails, screws or electricity — the way most North American buildings were built before about 1850. The House Building volume covers design; this section covers the joints and the green-woodworking methods.

6.1 Joinery

Cutting a mortise: bore a row of overlapping holes with an auger the width of the mortise, then pare the walls square with a chisel and mallet. Tenon: saw the shoulders across the grain, then split or rip the cheeks. Make the tenon about ⅓ the thickness of the beam and fit it snug but not forced. Draw-boring: drill the peg hole through the mortise walls, insert the tenon and mark the hole, pull it out and drill the tenon hole about ⅛ in (3 mm) closer to the shoulder. A tapered peg driven through pulls the shoulder tight and locks the joint for centuries.

6.2 Green woodworking

Freshly cut (green) wood is far easier to split, shave and carve than dry wood. The method: split (rive) rather than saw — split wood follows the grain, so it is stronger and does not break across short grain. Use wedges and a maul for logs, a froe and wooden club for boards, shingles (shakes) and handle blanks, and a riving brake (a forked log) to steer the split. Then shape on a shaving horse with drawknife and spokeshave, or hew with a broad axe and adze for beams.

6.3 Making pegs (trunnels)

Pegs (treenails, “trunnels”) replace nails in timber frames, furniture and boats. Split them from straight-grained, dry oak, locust, hickory or ash — never use sawn pegs, which break along short grain. Rive a billet into squares slightly larger than the hole, shave them to an octagon with a drawknife on the shaving horse, taper the leading end, and let them dry thoroughly. For timber frames use ¾–1 in (19–25 mm) pegs; for furniture ⅜–½ in (10–12 mm). A quick sizing tool: drive pegs through a hole of the right size in a steel plate (a dowel plate) to shave them round. Drive dry pegs into green or seasoned timber; they swell slightly and lock tight.

Drying woodAir-dry boards about 1 year per inch (25 mm) of thickness, stacked on stickers (spacers) under a roof with good airflow; seal the ends to stop checking (end cracks). Small parts like pegs and handle blanks dry faster near (not on) a stove. Combine dry tenons with green mortises: the mortise shrinks around the tenon and grips it — the trick behind chairs that last 200 years.

Part 7Safety in the Shop and at the Forge

In a grid-down world, an injury that would be minor today — a burn, an eye wound, a deep cut — can be disabling or fatal without hospitals or antibiotics. Treat these rules as non-negotiable.

BurnsMost forge burns come from picking up steel that looks cold — steel can be far above 400°F (200°C) with no glow. Rule: treat every piece of metal in a forge shop as hot; mark hot pieces, set them in one place, cool them in the slack tub before setting down. First aid (American Red Cross): stop the burning, cool the burn with cool (not ice-cold) clean running water for at least 10 minutes, remove rings and tight items, cover loosely with a sterile, non-stick dressing. Do not use butter, oil or ice; do not break blisters. Burns that are large, deep (white, leathery or charred), or on the face, hands, feet, groin or joints are serious — get the best medical help available.
Eye protectionWear impact-rated safety glasses (ANSI Z87.1 or CSA Z94.3) at the anvil, grinder, when chiseling, knapping, filing and striking hardened steel — hardened tool on hardened tool throws chips like shrapnel. Never strike two hammer faces together. Use a face shield over glasses for grinding and quenching, and proper filter shades for welding and cutting (OSHA eye and face protection guidance). Stockpile spare glasses.
Carbon monoxide (CO)Every fire — forge, charcoal kiln, stove, generator, engine — makes CO, an invisible, odorless gas that kills. Early symptoms are headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness and confusion, easily mistaken for flu or tiredness. Forge only outdoors or in an open-sided shed with a hood or chimney; never in a closed garage, basement or tent. Keep battery CO alarms in the shop and home. If anyone feels unwell, get everyone into fresh air immediately.
Galvanized metal: zinc fume fever — never heat galvanized steelGalvanized steel (fence posts and wire, pipe, roofing, buckets, garbage cans, trailer frames, many bolts) has a zinc coating. When heated, welded, cut or forged it releases white zinc oxide fumes. Breathing them causes metal fume fever: flu-like chills, fever, metallic taste, cough, nausea and aching starting hours later; heavy or repeated exposure is dangerous. Identify galvanizing by its dull gray, often crystalline “spangle” pattern. Remove it first by soaking in vinegar or muriatic acid outdoors until bubbling stops (with gloves and goggles), or simply leave galvanized material for cold uses. Also avoid heating cadmium-plated (yellowish) parts, brass and bronze (zinc), lead-painted metal, and anything coated with unknown paint or plastic.

Knapping stone: Ocala/Suwannee chert (silicified limestone, north-central Florida); agatized coral (Tampa Bay area; Florida’s state stone). Little else — glass is a substitute.

Climate: Highest humidity and salt air: rust is the main tool enemy — oil and wax everything, store in sealed boxes with dry rice or silica gel, keep off concrete. Heat: forge early and late; drink water.

8Desert SW & Great Basin

Wood (handles, charcoal): Scarce: mesquite (excellent dense charcoal and short handles), desert ironwood, Gambel oak and juniper at elevation, cottonwood along rivers (soft). Stockpile handles.

Salvage steel: Mining equipment and drill steel, ranches (springs, axles; barbed wire is galvanized), railroads, military surplus, abandoned vehicles everywhere (dry climate preserves them).

Knapping stone: Abundant obsidian and chert: Jemez Mountains (NM; Valles Caldera is protected), Mule Creek (NM), Government Mountain (near Flagstaff, AZ), Apache tears (Superior, AZ), Mineral Mountains/Black Rock area (UT); agate and jasper across Nevada.

Climate: Dry air shrinks handles and loosens heads — wedge tightly and oil wood often; tools rarely rust. Wildfire danger is extreme: forge only in cleared, wind-protected spots. Water for quenching and the slack tub is precious — cover it to stop evaporation.

9Rocky Mountains

Wood (handles, charcoal): Ash and oak are scarce; Rocky Mountain maple, chokecherry, serviceberry, Gambel oak (south), aspen (soft). Pine, fir and aspen charcoal (light, fast-burning); coal in WY, MT, CO, AB.

Salvage steel: Mining towns (rail, drill steel, cable, ore cars), ranches, railroads, logging equipment.

Knapping stone: Obsidian in Idaho (Malad, Big Southern Butte, Owyhee country) and Montana; Obsidian Cliff in Yellowstone (protected — no collecting); Spanish Diggings quartzite and Hartville chert (WY), Kremmling chert (CO).

Climate: Cold, dry, high altitude: handles shrink; steel brittle in deep cold; thin air — forges and engines need more air. Heavy snow on shop roofs. Short summers for charcoal making.

10California

Wood (handles, charcoal): Oregon/valley/coast live oak, Pacific madrone, California black walnut, ash in the north; eucalyptus (invasive, dense, cracks easily) and oak for charcoal.

Salvage steel: Huge urban scrap supply, auto yards, agriculture (vineyard, orchard and field equipment), ports and shipyards, rail.

Knapping stone: Obsidian: Glass Mountain/Medicine Lake (Modoc NF), Napa Valley, Bodie Hills; Coso (on a military base — off-limits). Franciscan chert (red radiolarian chert, Coast Ranges) and Monterey chert.

Climate: Long dry season with extreme wildfire risk — forge only in cleared areas with water at hand, ideally in the wet season. Coastal fog and salt air cause rust; inland heat.

11Northern Mexico

Wood (handles, charcoal): Mezquite (outstanding charcoal), encino (oak) and pine in the Sierra Madre; palo fierro (desert ironwood) and other dense desert woods for mallets and short handles.

Salvage steel: Truck and bus leaf springs (abundant — muelles), farm equipment in irrigated valleys, mining equipment (Sonora, Chihuahua, Zacatecas), railroads.

Knapping stone: Chert and chalcedony in the limestone ranges (Coahuila, Nuevo León); rhyolite and some obsidian in the volcanic Sierra Madre Occidental (Chihuahua, Durango). Fewer well-known sources — ask locally.

Climate: Heat and drought: work early morning; water is scarce for quenching. Monsoon storms bring flash floods in summer. Dry air loosens handles.

12Central & Southern Mexico

Wood (handles, charcoal): Encino (oak), pine, tropical hardwoods (e.g., guayacán, chicozapote) in the south; oak and mesquite charcoal in the highlands — charcoal is still widely made and sold.

Salvage steel: Vehicle springs and axles, sugar mills and agricultural machinery, rebar and construction steel, railroad lines; many working village blacksmiths (herreros) still exist.

Knapping stone: World-class obsidian: Sierra de las Navajas near Pachuca (green obsidian, Hidalgo), Otumba (State of Mexico), Ucareo–Zinapécuaro (Michoacán), Tequila/Teuchitlán (Jalisco), Pico de Orizaba (Veracruz–Puebla); chert across the Yucatán.

Climate: Rainy-season humidity causes rust; highlands are cooler and drier. Earthquakes — anchor anvils and tool racks. Protect forest: make charcoal from prunings and dead wood.

13Central America & Caribbean

Wood (handles, charcoal): Tropical hardwoods (guayacán / lignum vitae, very dense; madre de cacao; mahogany) and oak/pine in Guatemala and Honduras highlands; mangrove is traditional charcoal but should be protected — use Leucaena, Gliricidia and coffee prunings.

Salvage steel: Vehicles, sugar-mill machinery, rebar, boat hardware (stainless and galvanized), port and plantation equipment; on islands, salvage is limited — stockpile files and saws.

Knapping stone: Obsidian: El Chayal, Ixtepeque and San Martín Jilotepeque (Guatemala), Güinope (Honduras). Chert: Colha (Belize), Long Island flint (Antigua), chert in Cuba and Hispaniola.

Climate: Constant heat, humidity and salt air: rust and rot are the main enemies — oil, wax and store tools in sealed containers. Hurricanes: secure the shop and tools. Termites attack handles and wooden structures.
