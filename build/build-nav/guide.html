Part 1Maps

Land navigation combines map reading, dead reckoning (compass direction plus measured distance) and terrain association (matching the land to the map) [1]. Get paper topographic maps of your area and routes now; print several copies and keep them dry.

1.1 Reading a topographic map

  • Check the margin: scale, contour interval (height between lines), and the declination diagram showing true, grid and magnetic north [1].
  • The declination diagram is not drawn to scale — use the number written beside it [1].
  • Old maps may be out of date: new roads, missing bridges. Mark changes as you find them [1].

1.2 Orienting the map

A map is oriented when it lies flat with its north matching north on the ground [1].

  1. With a compass: lay the compass edge along a north–south grid line, then turn map and compass together until the needle points to north — then correct for declination (2.2) [1].
  2. By terrain association: find features you can see — a hill, a road, a river bend — and turn the map until they line up with the land [1].
Keep your thumb on itAs you walk, keep your thumb on your current position on the map so you never have to search the whole sheet [1].

Part 2The Compass

2.1 Azimuths and back azimuths

An azimuth is a direction measured clockwise from north, 0–360°. The back azimuth points the opposite way: add 180° if the azimuth is 180° or less; subtract 180° if it is more. Example: 115° → 295° [1]. Use back azimuths to return the way you came.

2.2 Declination: magnetic vs. true north

Declination is the angle between magnetic north (where the needle points) and true north; it is positive east and negative west, and changes over time and with location [3]. If you do not correct for it, directions between map and ground will be wrong by the full declination [1]. At 15°, that is about 270 m off course for every kilometer walked.

2.3 What throws a compass off

Keep the compass at least…from
55 mHigh-tension power lines [1]
18 mCars, trucks and heavy machinery [1]
10 mTelephone wires and barbed wire [1]
½ mA steel helmet or tools [1]

2.4 Bypassing obstacles and aiming off

  • Detour by right angles: on a 90° course, turn to 180° for 100 m, then 90° for 150 m, then 360° for 100 m, then back on 90° — you are back on your line [1].
  • Deliberate offset: when the goal is on a road or stream, aim about 10° to one side. Each degree moves you about 18 m sideways per 1,000 m; when you hit the line, you know which way to turn [1].
  • Handrails are linear features — trails, fences, roads, streams — you can follow quickly. Attack points are easy landmarks near your goal (stream junctions, bridges, road crossings) to navigate the last stretch from [1].

Part 3Distance: Pace Counting

3.1 Finding your pace count

A pace is one natural step, about 30 inches. Learn how many paces you take in 100 meters by walking a measured course (100–600 m) on ground like the ground you will travel [1]. Keep track with pebbles, knots in a string, or marks in a notebook — never by memory [1].

Part 4Without a Compass

The sun rises roughly east and sets roughly west, varying with the season. In the Northern Hemisphere the sun is due south at its highest point, and shadows move clockwise [2].

4.1 Shadow-stick method

  1. Push a straight stick about 1 m long into level ground; mark the tip of its shadow. The first mark is always west [2].
  2. Wait 10–15 minutes and mark the new tip [2].
  3. A line through the two marks runs roughly east–west [2].
  4. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right — you face north, anywhere on Earth [2].

More accurate: mark a morning shadow tip, draw an arc through it around the stick, and mark where the afternoon shadow touches the arc again; the line between is east–west [2].

4.2 Watch method

Northern Hemisphere: hold an analog watch flat, point the hour hand at the sun; halfway between the hour hand and 12 is south (the other end of that line is north). On daylight saving time, use halfway to 1 o'clock. It is more accurate farther from the equator [2]. With a digital watch, draw a clock face with the time on paper [2].

4.3 Stars: Polaris and the Southern Cross

  • Extend the line through the Big Dipper's two outer-lip "pointer" stars about five times their spacing to reach Polaris, the North Star [2].
  • Cassiopeia's W sits opposite the Dipper; Polaris lies straight out from its center star. Both circle Polaris counterclockwise and never set [2].
  • Polaris marks true north — no declination needed [2].
  • South of the equator (or low on the southern horizon in the tropics) the Southern Cross points south: extend its long axis about five times its length and look straight down to the horizon [2].

4.4 The moon, and improvised compasses

  • If the moon rises before sunset, its lit side faces west; if it rises after midnight, the lit side faces east [2].
  • Needle compass: stroke a needle or razor blade in one direction only with silk, through your hair, or with a magnet; hang it from a thread or float it on a leaf or wood chip in still water. It lines up north–south [2]. Check which end is north using the sun.
  • Moss on the north side is not reliable — it can grow all round a tree [2].

Part 5Traveling on Foot and by Bike

5.1 Planning a route

  • Study contour lines: follow ridges and valleys with gentle, evenly spaced contours; avoid tightly packed ones (cliffs) [1].
  • Choose handrails and attack points before you start; write the azimuth and pace count for each leg [1].
  • Plan water sources, shelter stops and bail-out routes. Travel in daylight when possible.
  • Tell someone your route and return time; leave a note at home with the date and direction you went.

5.2 Bicycles

  • A bicycle carries far more than a backpack and covers much more ground on roads and trails, with no fuel.
  • Carry a pump, spare tubes and patch kit, tire levers, multi-tool, chain lube and a spare chain link.
  • Load weight low on racks or panniers, not on your back. Walk the bike across fords, steep descents and debris.
  • Roads are the best bike handrails; plan around closed bridges and choke points using your map (1.1).

5.3 The go-bag

FEMA's basic kit list, adapted for carrying [5]:

Ready.gov basic kit [5]
  • Water — one gallon per person per day for several days (carry less, plus a filter and purification tablets)
  • Several days of non-perishable food; manual can opener
  • Battery or hand-crank radio and NOAA Weather Radio; flashlight; extra batteries
  • First aid kit; prescription medicines and glasses
  • Whistle to signal for help; dust mask
  • Plastic sheeting and duct tape; moist towelettes, garbage bags and ties
  • Wrench or pliers to turn off utilities
  • Local maps; phone with chargers and backup battery
  • Cash; copies of important documents in a waterproof container
  • Sleeping bag or warm blanket; a complete change of clothing
  • Matches in a waterproof container; hygiene supplies
Add for walking outA compass, your pace-count beads, sturdy broken-in shoes [5], rain gear, a hat, a knife, cordage, and a written list of meeting places and radio channels.

Part 6Crossing Water Safely

6.1 Choosing a crossing

Look from high ground (or climb a tree) before you cross [2].

Good places [2]Avoid [2]
Where the river splits into several narrow channelsLedges of rock across the river (rapids, canyons)
Upstream of a shallow bank or sandbar, so the current carries you to itJust above waterfalls or deep channels
A line angled downstream, crossing the current at about 45°Slick rocky beds; eddies that pull you under
Deep, slow water can be safer than fast shallow waterEstuaries — wide, strong, tidal currents
Very cold waterDo not swim or wade across a river when the water is very cold — it can be fatal. Build a raft, or wade only if just your feet get wet and dry them vigorously on the far bank [2].

6.2 Fording: alone and in a group

  • Keep footwear on for grip and to protect your feet [2]. Bundle gear on top of your pack and make sure you can slip the pack off instantly — a stuck pack can drown a strong swimmer [2].
  • Alone: use a strong pole, about 7.5 cm thick and 2.1–2.4 m long, planted on your upstream side; move the pole, then step below it, keeping it slanted so the current presses it against you [2].
  • Group: hold one pole parallel to the current with the heaviest person downstream and the lightest upstream; the upstream person breaks the current [2].
  • Rope: with three or more people and a rope three times the river's width, cross one at a time tied in and controlled by the others [2].
  • If swept away: in shallow rapids, float on your back, feet downstream and up; in deep rapids, swim on your stomach angling toward shore. Never fight the current [2].

6.3 Floodwater, bogs and quicksand

Turn around, don't drownJust 6 inches of fast-moving floodwater can knock over an adult; 12 inches carries away most cars and 2 feet carries away SUVs and trucks [6]. Never walk, bike or drive into moving floodwater.
  • Do not try to walk upright across bogs, muskeg or quicksand — lifting your feet makes you sink. Go around, or bridge with logs and branches [2].
  • If you must cross, lie face down with arms and legs spread and move slowly, pulling yourself along [2].
  • Test suspicious sand with a stone: it sinks in quicksand [2].
  • In swamps, vegetated areas are usually firmer than open mud [2].

Part 7Travel Hazards Region by Region

1Arctic & Subarctic
Declination is large and changes fast — about +14° to +15° in Anchorage and Yellowknife, about −24° at Iqaluit; near the magnetic pole compasses become unreliable [3,4]. Polaris is high overhead. Never wade very cold rivers; FM 21-76 warns it can be fatal — build a raft or find another way [2]. Whiteout, sea ice and long darkness: travel in daylight, pace-count, and handrail along coasts and rivers [1].
2Pacific NW & BC
Declination about +15° east [4]. Thick forest and cloud often hide sun and stars — rely on compass and handrails such as rivers and ridgelines [1]. Rain-swollen rivers and tidal estuaries are dangerous crossings [2].
3Prairies & Great Plains
Open, flat country with few landmarks: dead reckoning with compass and pace count matters [1]. Declination ranges from about +8° in Saskatchewan to near 0° in Kansas [4]. Blizzards and flash floods in creek beds [6].
4Great Lakes & Midwest
Declination a few degrees west (about −4° Chicago, −7.5° Detroit) [4]. Roads and section lines form a grid of handrails. Floodwater over roads: 6 inches of moving water can knock you down [6].
5Northeast & Atlantic Canada
West declination of −10° to −16° [4] — forget it and you will drift far off course. Rugged, forested hills; spring rivers run high and cold [2].
6Southeast & Gulf
Swamps, bayous and big rivers: cross where vegetation shows firm ground; avoid open mud [2]. Hurricane flooding: never walk or drive into moving water [6].
7Florida
Flat, low terrain and few viewpoints; climb a tree or building to plan routes [2]. Mangrove coasts: move at low tide and watch for crocodiles and alligators [2]. Declination about −7° [4].
8Desert SW & Great Basin
Flash floods in dry washes and slot canyons can arrive from storms far away [6]. Long distances between water. Declination about +10° [4].
9Rocky Mountains
Steep terrain and avalanches: most slab avalanches start on slopes of 30° to 50° [7] — stay off and out from under them in snow. Declination +7° to +13° [4]. Snowmelt rivers are fast and cold [2].
10California
Declination about +11° to +13° [4]. Mountain snow and avalanches in the Sierra [7]; flash floods in desert areas [6]; wildfire can close routes fast.
11Northern Mexico
Desert and sierra; arroyos flood suddenly [6]. Declination about +4° to +7° [4].
12Central & Southern Mexico
Highlands, volcanoes and deep barrancas — contour lines tell you where routes are possible [1]. Declination about +3° to +4° [4]. Rainy-season rivers rise quickly.
13Central America & Caribbean
Declination near 0° in Guatemala, about −6° in Cuba and −14° in Puerto Rico [4]. Jungle hides the sky: use compass and streams as handrails [1]; cross mangroves at low tide [2].
